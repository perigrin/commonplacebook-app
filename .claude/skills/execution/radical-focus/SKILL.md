# Radical Focus: OKR Execution System

## Overview

Radical Focus is not a goal-setting framework—it's an **execution system**. The methodology provides a complete weekly rhythm that keeps teams focused on ambitious goals despite constant distractions.

**Core Insight**: "If you say three things, you say nothing" (James Carville). Radical focus means ONE company objective per quarter, measured by three key results, tracked weekly with confidence levels, and executed through a commit/celebrate cadence.

**Three-Part System**:
1. **Set inspiring and measurable goals** - Unite company behind vision
2. **Ensure continuous progress** - Keep making progress despite distractions
3. **Establish a cadence** - Remember what you're trying to accomplish and hold each other accountable

## The "Radical" in Radical Focus

### ONE Objective Per Quarter

**The Rule**: ONE company Objective per quarter (not 2-3, not 5, not "top priorities"—ONE).

**Why This Is Radical**:
- Generic OKR advice suggests 2-3 objectives
- Google OKRs (Doerr) allows multiple objectives
- Wodtke insists: ONE or nothing

**Reasoning**:
- Can't remember five objectives
- Team gets confused about priorities
- No real focus if everything is "important"
- "If you say three things, you say nothing"

**Exception**: Multiple distinct business lines (e.g., Google running search + browser + social). Most companies and ALL startups: ONE.

### The 50% Confidence Rule

**The Rule**: All Key Results start at 5/10 confidence (50% chance of success).

**Not 70% Confidence** (common in Google OKRs):
- 70% leads to sandbagging
- Teams make 2 KRs easy, 1 impossibly hard
- Gaming the system instead of stretching

**Why 5/10**:
- "Hard goals are inspiring; impossible goals are depressing"
- Ensures stretch goals, not sandbag goals
- "I know I've got the right Key Results when you are also a little scared you can't make them"

**Expected Outcome**:
- Hit 2 of 3 KRs = good performance
- Hit all 3 = exceptional (maybe too easy next time)
- Hit 1 or 0 = learning opportunity (ask why, fix it)

## OKR Components

### Objective (O): The Qualitative Goal

**Definition**: What you want to do—the inspirational, qualitative goal.

**Characteristics**:
- Inspirational and motivating
- Qualitative (NO metrics)
- Makes you "leap out of bed eager to make it happen"
- Engages ALL team members (not just CFO/numbers people)
- Typically ONE per company per quarter

**Good Examples**:
- "Launch a killer game"
- "Become the platform for quality tea"
- "Establish clear value to restaurant suppliers as a quality tea provider"

**Bad Examples**:
- "Increase revenue by 25%" (metric-driven—belongs in KR)
- "Achieve $1M revenue with excellent customer satisfaction" (includes metrics)
- "Grow the business" (too vague, not inspiring)

**Critical Mistake to Avoid**: "The downfall of many an MBA"
- MBAs love numbers
- Not everyone does
- Metric-driven objectives only inspire finance people
- Designers, engineers, customer service need qualitative inspiration

### Key Results (KR): The Quantitative Measurements

**Definition**: How you know if you've achieved the Objective—the measurable outcomes.

**Characteristics**:
- Exactly **3 Key Results** per Objective (not 3-5, not 2-4—exactly 3)
- Numerical and measurable
- Start with 5/10 confidence for each
- Hard enough that you're "a little scared you can't make them"
- Results-based, not activity-based

**Recommended Balance** (Three Types of Metrics):
1. **Usage metric** (engagement, activity, adoption)
2. **Revenue metric** (financial performance)
3. **Satisfaction metric** (customer/user happiness)

**Why This Balance**: Different ways to measure success ensure sustained success across quarters. Not always appropriate, but good default.

**Good Examples**:
```
Objective: Establish clear value to restaurant suppliers as quality tea provider
  KR1: Reorders at 85% (5/10)
  KR2: Revenue of $250K (5/10)
  KR3: 20% of reorders self-serve (5/10)
```

**Bad Examples**:
- "Close 50 deals" (activity, not outcome)
- "Ship 10 features" (output, not result)
- "Launch new website" (task, not measurement)

**Writing KRs**:
1. Start with "X [metric]" (e.g., "X revenue", "X DAU")
2. Discuss WHAT to measure before setting values
3. Then set numerical targets
4. Challenge each other: Sandbagging? Too conservative? Foolhardy?
5. Ensure 5/10 confidence for each

### Confidence Levels: The Learning System

**Scale**: 1-10 for each Key Result

**Visual Signals**:
- **Red**: Confidence below 3/10 (investigate immediately)
- **Yellow**: 3-7/10 (normal range)
- **Green**: Above 7/10 (stay the course)

**Critical Practices**:
- **Track weekly** (don't go weeks at 5/10 without movement)
- **Update based on new information** (confidence should fluctuate)
- **Discuss changes** (Why did it move? What changed?)
- **Offer help when drops** (falling confidence triggers support)
- **Learn patterns** (who overestimates? who sandbags?)

**What Confidence Tracking Reveals**:
- Early warning system (problems surface early)
- Who overestimates consistently (needs to learn limits)
- Who sandbags consistently (needs to stretch more)
- What conditions allow success vs what blocks it
- When to offer help vs when to celebrate

**If Confidence Never Changes**: Team forgot about OKRs, not paying attention, missing the point entirely.

## The Four Steps Framework

### Step 1: SET (Setting OKRs)

**Timeline**: 2 weeks total (if longer, examine your priorities—nothing more critical than setting a goal to rally behind)

#### Day 1: Employee Input (24 hours)

**Process**:
- All employees submit Objective suggestions
- Short window (24 hours) prevents delays
- Delivered to executive team

**Benefits**:
- Increases buy-in
- Provides insight into company culture health
- "We value the things we make together"

#### Day 2-3: Executive Team OKR Setting

**Duration**: 4.5 hours scheduled (goal: finish in 2 hours)

**Participants**: CEO (leading), senior execs, ≤10 people

**Preparation**: Each exec brings 1-2 Objectives; remove phones/computers

**Meeting Structure**:

1. **Post-it Technique**: Write all Objectives on large Post-its, place on wall
2. **Combine duplicates**: Look for patterns, stack rank
3. **Debate**: Fight if needed—this deserves time
4. **Choose ONE**: Narrow to three, then pick ONE company Objective
5. **Freelist Metrics** (Design Thinking technique): Everyone writes metric ideas on Post-its (10 min)
6. **Affinity Map**: Group similar metrics together
7. **Select Three Types**: Identify usage, revenue, satisfaction (or appropriate alternatives)
8. **Write "X [metric]" First**: Discuss WHAT before VALUE
9. **Set Numerical Values**: "Shoot for the moon"
10. **Challenge Each Other**: Sandbagging? Conservative? Foolhardy?
11. **Ensure 5/10 Confidence**: For each KR
12. **Final Review** (5 min): Can you live with this for full quarter?

**Questions for Final Review**:
- Is Objective aspirational and inspirational?
- Do KRs make sense?
- Are they hard?
- Can you live with this for a full quarter?

#### Day 3-7: Department OKR Development

**Process**:
- Execs introduce company OKR to direct reports
- Departments develop their own OKRs (2-hour meetings)
- Same process: freelist, group, stack rank, select
- NOT dictated—collaboratively created

#### Day 8-9: CEO Review and Approval

**Duration**: 1 day focused only on this

**Focus**: Ensuring alignment without dictating

**Coaching Questions**:
- How does this contribute to company goal?
- Are these Key Results measurable?
- Outcome-focused vs activity-focused?
- Are you sandbagging or being foolhardy?

#### Day 14-15: All-Hands Rollout

**Content**:
- CEO explains WHY this OKR for this quarter
- Call out exemplary department OKRs
- Review last quarter's OKRs and key wins
- Keep tone positive and determined

### Step 2: COMMUNICATE (Alignment and Buy-In)

#### Making OKRs Visible

- Public within organization
- Discussed weekly in team meetings
- Referenced in status emails
- Present in all planning conversations

#### For Product Organizations: Critical Cascade Rule

**The Problem**: Functional departments (Engineering, Design, QA) create their own OKRs while individuals serve on cross-functional product teams.

**Result**: Engineer told to work on technical debt (Eng OKR), designer told responsive design (Design OKR), but product team needs them for DAU increase (Product OKR). **Conflicted team members, confusion, disappointing results.**

**The Solution**:
- **Product team members' primary OKRs come from their product team** (where they work)
- NOT from functional department (where they report)
- Functional managers CAN have strategic OKRs (no conflict—they're not on product teams)
- Larger functional objectives incorporated into relevant product team OKRs

**Cascade Levels**:
- **Level 1**: Company OKR (1 per quarter)
- **Level 2**: Product team OKRs (where cross-functional teams work)
- **Level 3**: Individual OKRs (optional, supporting product team)

**Critical Principle**: Focus OKRs at the product team level, NOT functional department level.

### Step 3: TRACK (Weekly Monitoring)

#### Monday Commitments Meeting: The Four-Square Framework

The four-square is Wodtke's distinctive structure—NOT found in generic OKR literature.

**Structure** (Four Quadrants):

```
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
|   THIS WEEK'S PRIORITIES  |      OKRs & CONFIDENCE   |
|                           |                           |
| P1: [Must do task]        | Objective: [Inspiring     |
| P1: [Must do task]        |            goal]          |
| P1: [Must do task]        |                           |
| P2: [Should do task]      | KR1: [Metric] [Value]     |
|                           |      Confidence: 5/10     |
| 3-4 most important things | KR2: [Metric] [Value]     |
| Focus on what must happen |      Confidence: 5/10     |
| to move OKRs forward      | KR3: [Metric] [Value]     |
|                           |      Confidence: 5/10     |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
|    FORECAST / HEADS UP    |     HEALTH METRICS        |
|                           |                           |
| - What's coming in next   | Team Health: [R/Y/G]      |
|   4 weeks                 |                           |
| - Big launches            | Customer Satisfaction:    |
| - Coordination needed     |      [R/Y/G]              |
|                           |                           |
| Help teams prepare and    | 2 things to protect while |
| coordinate                | shooting for the moon     |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
```

**Upper Left - This Week's Priorities**:
- 3-4 most important things (P1: must do)
- These are TRUE COMMITMENTS (different from aspirational OKRs)
- Don't list everything—just what matters most

**Upper Right - OKRs & Confidence**:
- List company/team OKRs
- Show confidence levels (5/10 format)
- Update confidence weekly
- Discuss why confidence moves

**Lower Left - Forecast/Heads Up**:
- What's coming in next 4 weeks
- Big things on horizon
- Help teams prepare/coordinate

**Lower Right - Health Metrics**:
- 2 things to protect while shooting for moon
- Examples: team well-being, customer satisfaction, code stability
- Mark Red/Yellow/Green
- Forces discussion about what's being protected

**Meeting Guidelines**:
- **Close laptops, full attention**
- **Conversation tool, NOT status report**
- **Time allocation**: 25% presentations, 75% discussion
- **Key questions**:
  - Do priorities lead to hitting OKRs?
  - Why is confidence dropping or rising?
  - Are we prepared for major new efforts?
  - Are we burning out or letting quality slip?
- **Trust team makes good everyday choices**
- About big stuff needing coordination/help

#### Confidence Tracking Practices

**Weekly Updates**:
- Mark confidence changes every week
- Don't go weeks at 5/10 without movement (means not paying attention)
- Dropping from 8/10 to 2/10? Investigate immediately—what changed?
- Rising to 8/10? Good sign—stay the course

**When Confidence Changes**:
- **Drops**: Offer help, investigate blockers
- **Rises**: Celebrate, validate approach
- **Stagnant**: Team forgot about OKRs

**What Movement Reveals**:
- What's working and what's not
- Who overestimates (needs to learn limits)
- Who sandbags (needs to stretch more)
- When to offer support vs when to stay out of way

### Step 4: STRETCH (Learning and Adjusting)

#### Two Weeks Before Quarter End

**Honest Assessment**:
- Will we hit OKRs?
- Don't lie about pulling rabbits out of hats
- Admit missed KRs or KRs set too low
- Extract the learning

#### Learning Focus

**OKRs Are About Learning Cycles, NOT Check Marks**:

"So you didn't hit any of your KRs. Ask yourself why, and fix it. So you hit them all? Set harder goals, and move on. Focus on learning, getting smarter and having better things to celebrate every Friday."

**Key Questions**:
- Why did confidence move?
- What changed our trajectory?
- What conditions allow success?
- What keeps us from getting things done?
- Two camps: overestimators (learn limits) vs sandbaggers (need to stretch)

**What to Celebrate**:
- What setting big goals enabled you to accomplish
- Even if KRs weren't fully hit
- Progress made, not just final binary outcome

#### Adjustment for Next Quarter

- Start OKR-setting with learnings in mind
- Use confidence tracking insights
- Address patterns that emerged
- Apply lessons to next quarter's goals

## The Weekly Rhythm: Commit/Celebrate Cadence

### Monday: Commitments (The Tough Day)

**Purpose**: Public commitment to progress toward Objective

**Format**: Four-square framework (detailed above)

**Tone**: Trust team's decisions, focus on helping each other meet shared goals, about big stuff needing coordination

### Friday: Wins (The Celebration)

**Purpose**: Celebrate progress and build winning team culture

**Critical Importance**: "When aiming high, teams fail a lot. Seeing how far you've come prevents depression."

**Structure**:
- **Everyone demos something**:
  - Engineers: bits of code working
  - Designers: mockups and maps
  - Sales: who they closed
  - Customer Service: customers rescued
  - Business Development: deals in progress
  - Every team shares something

**Benefits**:
1. Feel part of special winning team
2. Team looks forward to having something to share (seeks wins during week)
3. Company appreciates what each discipline does
4. Cross-functional understanding grows

**Celebration Elements**:
- Provide beer, wine, cake, or treats
- Keep upbeat—antidote to Monday's grim business
- Celebrate what WAS accomplished
- Build morale for hard push toward ambitious goals

**Critical Principle**: "Be tough on Monday, celebrate on Friday"

**Why This Works**:
- Commit/celebrate cadence creates habit of execution
- Bookends of high-performing team's week
- Balances stretch goal difficulty with progress recognition

### The Weekly Rhythm Is the Execution System

**Key Insight**: No system works if you don't actually keep to it.

Setting a goal at the beginning of a quarter and expecting it to magically be achieved is naive. The weekly rhythm IS the system.

## Critical Distinctions

### Distinction 1: Commitments vs Aspirational Goals

**Quarterly OKRs = ASPIRATIONAL** (stretch goals, 5/10 confidence)
**Weekly P1 Priorities = COMMITMENTS** (must be accomplished)

**This distinction is crucial and completely missing from generic OKR knowledge.**

**Why It Matters**:
- Different psychological contracts
- Weekly P1s connect daily work (committed) to big goals (aspirational)
- OKRs inspire; P1s execute
- Don't treat OKRs as pass/fail checklist

### Distinction 2: Inspirational vs Metric-Driven

**Objective = Qualitative, Inspirational** (leaps out of bed)
**Key Results = Quantitative, Measurable** (know if you succeeded)

**Common Mistake**: Including metrics in Objective
- Bad: "Achieve $1M revenue with excellent customer satisfaction"
- Good: "Become the platform for quality tea" (O) + "$250K revenue" (KR)

### Distinction 3: Activity vs Outcome

**Activities** (Bad KRs):
- "Close 50 deals"
- "Ship 10 features"
- "Launch new website"

**Outcomes** (Good KRs):
- "Revenue of $250K"
- "Reorders at 85%"
- "20% of reorders self-serve"

**Why Outcomes Matter**: Activities don't guarantee results. Focus on what you're trying to achieve, not tasks to complete.

### Distinction 4: OKRs as Execution System, Not Goal-Setting

**Generic View**: OKRs are a goal-setting framework

**Wodtke's View**: OKRs are a complete execution system

**What Makes It an Execution System**:
- Weekly rhythm (Monday commitments, Friday wins)
- Confidence tracking (management tool)
- Four-square framework (coordination and support)
- Learning cycles (continuous improvement)

**The goals themselves matter less than**:
- Focus they create
- Conversations they enable
- Commitments they forge
- Learning they generate
- Celebration they inspire

### Distinction 5: Time Frame (Quarterly, Not Shorter)

**Bad Idea**: Monthly or weekly OKRs

**Why It Fails**: "Nothing truly bold can be done in a week or month. If it can be done in a week, it's just a task."

**Proper Time Frame**: Full three months (quarter)

**Question to Ask**: "What truly bold thing can be accomplished in less?"

**Exception**: Pre-product/market fit startups
- Might not be ready for OKRs
- Exception: OKR could be "Find product/market fit"
- If can't keep on track longer than a week, probably not ready

## Common Mistakes (Wodtke's Specific List)

### Mistake 1: Too Many OKRs Per Quarter

**Error**: 5 Objectives with 15 total KRs

**Why It Fails**: Can't remember them all; team gets confused; no focus

**Fix**: ONE company Objective per quarter (unless multiple distinct business lines)

### Mistake 2: Metric-Driven Objective

**Error**: "Increase revenue by 25%" as Objective

**Why It Fails**: Not inspirational for non-numbers people

**Fix**: Qualitative Objective ("Launch a killer game") + metrics in KRs

**Quote**: "The downfall of many an MBA"—loving numbers doesn't mean everyone does

### Mistake 3: Too Short Time Frame

**Error**: Weekly or monthly OKRs

**Why It Fails**: Nothing bold in a week; just tasks

**Fix**: Commit to full quarter

### Mistake 4: Not Setting Confidence Levels

**Error**: No confidence tracking, or expecting 70% achievement

**Why It Fails**: Sandbagging 2 KRs, making 1 impossibly hard; gaming the system

**Fix**: All KRs start at 5/10 confidence

### Mistake 5: Not Tracking Changing Confidence

**Error**: Set confidence once, never update

**Why It Fails**: No early warning; miss opportunity to help

**Fix**: Mark confidence weekly; offer help when drops; discuss why it moves

### Mistake 6: Four-Square as Status Report

**Error**: Everyone presents, meeting ends—no discussion

**Why It Fails**: No coordination, no support, no real conversation

**Fix**: 75% discussion time; ask key questions about priorities, confidence, health

### Mistake 7: Being Tough on Friday

**Error**: Using Friday wins for criticism or problem discussion

**Why It Fails**: Teams need celebration to counterbalance hard push; demoralizes

**Fix**: Save tough for Monday; Friday is for toasting accomplishments

### Mistake 8: Including Metrics in Objective

**Error**: "Achieve $1M revenue with excellent customer satisfaction" as Objective

**Why It Fails**: Confuses O and KR; not inspirational

**Fix**: Keep Objective inspirational; metrics only in KRs

### Mistake 9: Activity-Based KRs

**Error**: "Close 50 deals", "Ship 10 features"

**Why It Fails**: Measures activities, not outcomes

**Fix**: Focus on results—what those activities should achieve

### Mistake 10: Top-Down Dictation

**Error**: CEO sets OKR over weekend, announces Monday

**Why It Fails**: No buy-in; missing insights; resistance

**Fix**: Employee input (24 hours) → exec collaboration (4.5 hours) → departmental development (2 hours) → CEO approval → all-hands explanation

**Quote**: "We value the things we make together"

### Mistake 11: Not Setting Aside Time

**Error**: Rushing the process

**Why It Fails**: Company goal deserves time; nothing more critical

**Fix**: Schedule 4.5 hours; do preparation; remove phones; make decisions in room

### Mistake 12: Treating as Pass/Fail

**Error**: Hit only 1 of 3 KRs → CEO disappointed → team morale drops

**Why It Fails**: Misses the point; creates fear; loses continuous improvement

**Fix**: Hit 2 of 3 = good; all 3 = exceptional; 1 or 0 = learning opportunity

**Key Understanding**: OKRs are about continuous improvement and learning cycles, NOT check marks

## When to Use This Methodology

### Ideal Contexts

**Organizational Readiness**:
- After achieving product/market fit
- When company needs focus amid distractions
- When coordinating multiple teams/departments
- When rapid and sustained growth is goal
- When team tends toward "shiny object syndrome"

**Team Size**: Works for any size
- Small teams: Company-only OKR, simple four-square
- Large teams: Full cascade with department/product team OKRs
- Distributed teams: Weekly rhythm creates alignment

### When NOT to Use (or Use Cautiously)

**Pre-Product/Market Fit Startups**:
- Might not be ready for quarterly goals
- Exception: OKR could be "Find product/market fit"
- If can't keep on track longer than a week, not ready

**Highly Variable Work**:
- Emergency services, consulting with unpredictable project flow
- Consider project-level OKRs instead of quarterly

### First-Time Implementation: Three Options

**Option 1 - Start with Company-Only OKR**:
- Quarter 1: Only company OKR, don't cascade
- Exec team models high standards
- See who adopts naturally
- Quarter 2: Cascade to departments

**Option 2 - Pilot with One Team**:
- Quarter 1: One independent team adopts
- Team should have all skills to achieve goals
- Quarter 2+: Expand after proving it works

**Option 3 - Start with Projects**:
- Before quarterly OKRs: Apply to major projects only
- Every project asks: "What's the Objective?" and "How will we know we succeeded?"
- Builds Objective-Result thinking habit
- Then move to quarterly company OKRs

### For Coaches and Advisors

### The Formula

**Company Level**: 1 Objective + 3 Key Results

**Example**:

### The Non-Negotiables

1. **ONE** company Objective per quarter
2. Exactly **3** Key Results
3. **5/10** confidence for each KR at start
4. **Quarterly** time frame (3 months)
5. **Weekly** confidence updates
6. **Four-square** Monday commitments
7. **Friday** wins celebration
8. **2-week** OKR setting process with employee input
9. **Inspirational** Objective (qualitative)
10. **Measurable** KRs (quantitative)

- **Monday**: Commitments meeting (four-square, 75% discussion)
- **During Week**: Execute P1 priorities, track confidence changes

### The Confidence Scale

- **10/10**: Already done (too easy)
- **8-9/10**: Very likely (GREEN—stay the course)
- **5-6/10**: 50/50 shot (YELLOW—target starting point)
- **3-4/10**: Unlikely (YELLOW—discuss what's causing doubt)
- **2/10**: Very unlikely (RED—investigate immediately)
- **1/10**: No way (RED—may need to rethink)

## Practice Exercises

### Exercise 1: Fixing Bad Objectives

Transform these metric-driven Objectives into inspirational ones:

1. "Achieve $2M revenue with 95% customer satisfaction"
2. "Increase market share by 15%"
3. "Reduce churn to 3% and grow userbase 50%"

**Answer Pattern**: Remove all metrics (move to KRs). Ask: "What's the qualitative vision this serves?" Make it inspirational for all team members.

### Exercise 2: Identifying Activity vs Outcome KRs

Mark each as Activity (A) or Outcome (O):
1. "Launch 15 new features"
2. "Achieve 10K daily active users"
3. "Run 50 sales meetings"
4. "Close $500K in new contracts"
5. "Ship responsive design"
6. "Increase mobile engagement to 40% of sessions"

**Answers**: 1=A, 2=O, 3=A, 4=O, 5=A, 6=O

**Fix Activities**: Ask "What result should this activity achieve?"

### Exercise 3: Four-Square for Your Current Work

Create a four-square for your current week:
- Upper left: 3-4 P1 priorities
- Upper right: Your OKRs with confidence
- Lower left: What's coming in 4 weeks
- Lower right: 2 health metrics to protect

**Then ask**:
- Do my priorities move my KRs forward?
- Why is my confidence where it is?
- What do I need help with?
- Are my health metrics okay?

### Exercise 4: Confidence Movement Analysis

A team's KR confidence over 12 weeks:
- Week 1: 5/10
- Week 4: 5/10
- Week 8: 5/10
- Week 12: 3/10

**Questions**:
1. What's the problem?
2. When should intervention happen?
3. What does this pattern reveal?

**Answers**: Team not paying attention (no movement Weeks 1-8). Should intervene by Week 4 ("don't go weeks at 5/10"). Reveals team forgot about OKRs or isn't working toward them.

### Exercise 5: Cascade for Product Organizations

**Scenario**: Company with 3 product teams. Engineering, Design, and QA as functional departments. Engineers/designers serve on product teams but report to functional managers.

**Question**: Where should individual contributors' primary OKRs come from?

**Answer**: Product team (where they work), NOT functional department (where they report). Functional managers can have strategic OKRs, but they don't conflict because managers aren't on product teams.

### Exercise 6: Hit Rate Interpretation

**Quarter End Results**:
- Team A: Hit 3 of 3 KRs
- Team B: Hit 2 of 3 KRs
- Team C: Hit 0 of 3 KRs

**Questions**:
1. Which team performed well?
2. Which team might have sandbagged?
3. Which team needs investigation?

**Answers**:
1. Team B performed well (hitting 2 of 3 is good)
2. Team A might have sandbagged (all 3 suggests too easy)
3. Team C needs investigation (0 of 3 means either too ambitious or wrong goals—ask why, fix it)

### Exercise 7: Time Frame Decision

**Scenarios**: Which should use quarterly OKRs?
1. Pre-product/market fit startup pivoting weekly
2. Series B company scaling sales and product
3. Established company launching new product line
4. Crisis mode startup running out of runway

**Answers**: 2 and 3 are good fits. 1 might not be ready (exception: "Find product/market fit"). 4 might need shorter tactical cycles, but quarterly OKR could still provide direction.

### Exercise 8: Employee Input Simulation

**Exercise**: Collect Objective suggestions from your team (24-hour window).

**Then analyze**:
- What patterns emerged?
- What does this reveal about culture/priorities?
- How could these combine into ONE company Objective?

**Practice**: Affinity mapping—group similar suggestions, look for themes.

### Exercise 9: Metric Type Classification

Classify each metric as Usage (U), Revenue (R), or Satisfaction (S):
1. Daily active users
2. Net Promoter Score
3. Monthly recurring revenue
4. Engagement rate
5. Customer lifetime value
6. Support ticket resolution time
7. Feature adoption rate

**Answers**: 1=U, 2=S, 3=R, 4=U, 5=R, 6=S, 7=U

**Practice**: For your current Objective, select one metric from each category for balanced measurement.

### Exercise 10: Monday Meeting Simulation

**Setup**: Facilitate a Monday commitment meeting with these rules:
- Everyone prepares four-square
- Close laptops
- Present four-squares (25% of time)
- Discuss (75% of time)

**Discussion Questions**:
- Do priorities move KRs?
- Why is confidence changing?
- Who needs help?
- Are health metrics okay?

**Debrief**: Did discussion reveal anything presentations wouldn't? Did 75% discussion feel right?

## Integration with Other Methodologies

### With Agile/Scrum
- Weekly rhythm borrows from Scrum commitment patterns
- Four-square = "Scrum of scrums" for multidisciplinary teams
- P1 priorities = sprint commitments
- OKRs = release goals

### With Design Thinking
- Freelist technique for generating metrics
- Affinity mapping for grouping ideas
- Post-it wall for collaborative selection
- "We value the things we make together"

### With Theory of Constraints (TOC)
- ONE objective = TOC's focus on single constraint
- Health metrics = protecting throughput while optimizing constraint
- Confidence tracking = identifying blockers early
- Friday wins = celebrating throughput improvements

### With Thinking in Bets
- 5/10 confidence = probabilistic thinking
- Learning focus = outcome fielding
- Confidence tracking = updating beliefs with new information
- "Hit 2 of 3 = good" = accepting variance in results

## Final Wisdom

**The Core Insight**: OKRs are not a goal-setting tool. They are an execution system.

**What Makes It Work**:
- The focus ONE objective creates
- The conversations weekly rhythm enables
- The commitments four-square forges
- The learning confidence tracking generates
- The celebration Friday wins inspires

**Starting Point**: "Ideas aren't worth the paper they're printed on. It's not important to protect an idea. It's important to protect the time it takes to make it real."

**The System**: Radical Focus provides that protection—a system to keep teams aimed at their goals when the world throws distractions at them.

**Success Indicator**: "Starting every week with a public setting of priorities is powerful. You commit to the team and to each other to make the Objective occur. A Friday celebration of what's been accomplished is the second bookend of a high-performing team's week. This commit/celebrate cadence creates a habit of execution."
