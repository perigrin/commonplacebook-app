# Thinking in Bets: Practical Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

## Core Philosophy

### Life is Poker, Not Chess

**The Fundamental Mistake:** We treat life like chess—a game of perfect information where outcomes directly reflect decision quality. Reality is poker—hidden information, significant luck, and fundamental uncertainty.

**Chess characteristics:**
- Complete information available
- Outcomes perfectly correlate with decisions
- Better player nearly always wins
- Wrong decisions immediately identifiable

**Poker characteristics (Reality):**
- Incomplete information
- Hidden variables
- Luck significantly influences outcomes
- Quality decisions can yield bad results
- Bad decisions can yield good results

> "Life is more like poker. You could make the smartest, most careful decision in firing a company president and still have it blow up in your face. You could run a red light and get through the intersection safely—or follow all the traffic rules and signals and end up in an accident."

**The Equation:**
Quality of life = Decision quality + Luck

We must learn to separate the two.

## Resulting: The Cardinal Sin

### Definition and Recognition

**Resulting** is equating the quality of a decision with the quality of its outcome—judging decisions solely by how they turned out rather than the quality of the decision-making process at the time.

**Warning Signs:**
- "That was a terrible decision" (after bad outcome)
- "That was brilliant" (after good outcome)
- "I knew it would work"
- "I should have known better"

### The Pete Carroll Deep-Dive

Super Bowl XLIX, 2015: The most dissected play call in NFL history.

**The Situation:**
- 26 seconds remaining
- Second-and-goal at the 1-yard line
- Seahawks trailing by 4
- One timeout left

**The Call:** Pass play instead of running with Marshawn Lynch

**The Result:** Intercepted. Game over. Headlines: "Worst Play Call in Super Bowl History."

**The Reality of the Decision Tree:**

**If Carroll Runs:**
- Probability of touchdown: ~50%
- Probability of fumble: 1-2%
- Most likely outcome: Stopped short
- If stopped short: MUST use final timeout
- Result: Only 1 more play possible (3rd down)
- Total chances to score: 2

**If Carroll Passes:**
- Probability of touchdown: ~45%
- Probability of interception: 2-3%
- Most likely outcome: Incomplete pass
- If incomplete: Clock stops automatically
- Still have timeout remaining
- Result: Can run 2 more plays (3rd and 4th down)
- Total chances to score: 3

**The Decision Quality:**
- Pass gave THREE chances vs. TWO chances
- Small additional risk: 2-3% interception vs. 1-2% fumble
- Massive benefit: Extra play attempt
- Sound process: Maximize scoring opportunities

**What Happened:**
The 2-3% outcome occurred. This doesn't make the decision wrong—it makes the outcome unlucky.

**Key Insight:** Even most analysts missed the "three plays vs. two plays" advantage after having time to review. This demonstrates how resulting clouds even expert analysis.

> "A good decision is the result of a good process, and that process must include an attempt to accurately represent our own state of knowledge."

### Other Examples of Resulting

**Phil Hellmuth (World Series of Poker record holder):**
> "If it weren't for luck, I'd win every one."

Translation: "All my losses are luck, all my wins are skill."

**Auto Insurance Claims (Duke's collection):**
- "I collided with a stationary truck coming the other way"
- "An invisible car came out of nowhere"
- "The telephone pole was approaching"
- Single-vehicle accidents: 37% blamed someone else

**The Steve Bartman Effect:**
2003 Cubs game, one out from World Series:
- Foul ball, multiple fans reached
- Bartman touched it, Alou couldn't catch
- Cubs then gave up 8 runs, lost game and series
- Death threats, assault, blame for everything

**The Reality:**
- Several fans reached simultaneously
- Cubs still had lead after the play
- Shortstop error on double-play ball
- Ace pitcher gave up multiple hits
- Still had another game to win series

One small event (low probability) gets blamed for entire outcome (many contributors).

## Thinking in Bets Framework

### All Decisions Are Bets

Merriam-Webster definition of "bet":
- "A choice made by thinking about what will probably happen"
- "To risk losing (something) when you try to do or achieve something"
- "To make decisions that are based on the belief that something will happen or is true"

Every decision involves:
- **Choice** among alternatives
- **Probability** assessment of outcomes
- **Risk** of being wrong
- **Belief** about what will happen
- Commitment of resources (time, money, reputation, happiness)

### Betting Against Ourselves

Most decisions aren't zero-sum against others—we're betting against alternative versions of our future selves:
- Taking Job A vs. staying in current job
- Going to the movies vs. spending time another way
- Eating the cookie vs. eating the apple

Each choice bets that the future-self resulting from that decision will be better off.

### The Power of "Wanna Bet?"

When someone challenges us with "Wanna bet?" it triggers immediate examination:
- How do I know this?
- Where did I get this information?
- Who did I get it from?
- What is the quality of my sources?
- How much do I trust them?
- How up to date is my information?
- What other things like this have I been confident about that turned out not to be true?
- What are the other plausible alternatives?
- What am I missing?

**Practice:** Start using "Wanna bet?" with yourself on your own beliefs.

### Expressing Uncertainty: The 0-10 Scale

**The Cultural Problem:**
- Schools mark "I don't know" as wrong
- Society rewards appearing certain
- Movie stars "want to be the man who knows"
- We're conditioned to pretend certainty

**The Scientific Reality:**
> "Thoroughly conscious ignorance is the prelude to every real advance in science." —James Clerk Maxwell

**The Better Way:**
Rate beliefs on 0-10 scale:
- 0 = certain belief is false
- 10 = certain belief is true
- Most beliefs fall somewhere between

**Examples:**
- "I'm 60% sure Citizen Kane won Best Picture"
- "Somewhere between 40 and 47" (for Elvis's age at death)

**Benefits of Expressing Uncertainty:**

1. **More accurate:** Reflects actual state of knowledge
2. **Reduces motivated reasoning:** Small adjustments feel better than right/wrong reversal
3. **Increases credibility:** Thoughtful uncertainty is more believable than false certainty
4. **Invites collaboration:** Others feel comfortable contributing information
5. **Serves listeners:** Signals information needs vetting
6. **Enables learning:** Creates opportunities to calibrate beliefs

**Redefining Wrong:**
If you assessed 76% probability and the 24% happened, you weren't "wrong"—you made an accurate probability assessment and the less likely outcome occurred.

## Brain Architecture: Understanding the Hardware

### The Two Systems

**Reflexive Mind (System 1):**
- Fast, automatic, unconscious
- Evolutionarily older brain regions (cerebellum, basal ganglia, amygdala)
- Pattern recognition for survival
- Handles ~80% of daily decisions
- Prone to cognitive shortcuts and biases

**Deliberative Mind (System 2):**
- Slow, effortful, conscious
- Prefrontal cortex
- Analytical, rational processing
- Limited capacity—"already overtaxed"
- Required for complex strategic decisions

**The Challenge:**
> "We have this thin layer of prefrontal cortex made just for us, sitting on top of this big animal brain. Getting this thin little layer to handle more is unrealistic."

### Evolution's Trade-offs

Our brains evolved for:
- **Certainty over accuracy:** Better to assume rustling = lion (false positive) than assume rustling = wind when it's a lion (false negative)
- **Speed over precision:** Quick decisions keep us alive
- **Pattern recognition over probabilistic thinking:** "This always happens" feels more comfortable than "This happens X% of the time"

**Implication:** We can't fundamentally change our brain architecture, but we can:
1. Recognize when we're in reflexive mode
2. Create systems that trigger deliberative thinking
3. Build better habits that run automatically

### Belief Formation: The Backwards Process

**How We Think It Works:**
1. Hear something
2. Think about it and vet it
3. Form belief

**How It Actually Works:**
1. Hear something
2. **Believe it immediately**
3. Maybe later, if time/inclination, vet it

**Why This Evolved:**
- For direct sensory experience (seeing a tree), believing makes sense
- When language evolved, we applied the same system to secondhand information
- "Nature does not start from scratch; rather, she is an inveterate jury rigger"

**Common False Beliefs:**
- Baldness from maternal grandfather (oversimplified)
- Dog years = human years × 7 (complete fabrication)
- We use only 10% of our brain (false)
- Ellis Island officials changed immigrants' names (false)

**The Cost:** Same process applies to consequential beliefs about diet, medicine, politics, business strategy, relationships.

## Motivated Reasoning and Confirmation Bias

### The Mechanism

Once beliefs form, we:
1. Notice and seek confirming evidence
2. Rarely challenge validity of confirming evidence
3. Ignore or actively discredit contradicting evidence

> "Instead of altering our beliefs to fit new information, we do the opposite, altering our interpretation of that information to fit our beliefs."

### "They Saw a Game" Study

**The 1951 Dartmouth-Princeton Game:**
- Same game, same film
- Princeton students: Dartmouth committed twice as many flagrant penalties
- Dartmouth students: Both teams equal in infractions

**Conclusion:** "We do not simply 'react to' a happening. . . . We behave according to what we bring to the occasion."

### Smart People Are Worse

**The Blind-Spot Bias Study:**
- Testing cognitive ability vs. recognition of bias
- Results: Higher cognitive ability = **LARGER** blind spots
- Smarter people are better at constructing narratives supporting their beliefs
- "Nobody has found a way to eradicate confirmation bias in individuals"

**Dan Kahan's Gun Control Study:**
- Same data presented as either skin treatment or gun control effects
- On neutral topic (skin cream): numeracy predicted accuracy
- On charged topic (guns): numeracy predicted **MORE bias**
- More numerate people better at spinning numbers to fit beliefs

**Implication:** You can't think your way out of bias individually. You need systems and groups.

## Self-Serving Bias: The Outcome-Fielding Problem

### The Pattern

- **Good outcomes →** My skill
- **Bad outcomes →** Bad luck
- **My success →** Superior decision-making
- **My failure →** Circumstances beyond control
- **Others' success →** They got lucky
- **Others' failure →** Their poor decisions

### The Cost

**Blaming bad outcomes on luck** = Missing learning opportunities
**Taking credit for lucky good outcomes** = Reinforcing poor decisions

> "Being wrong hurts us more than being right feels good. . . . losses feel about two times as bad as wins feel good."

### The Learning Loop

```
Beliefs → Decisions → Outcomes → Feedback
            ↑                    ↓
            └──── Learning ──────┘
```

**The Challenge:** Outcomes are rarely 100% luck or 100% skill.

### The SnackWell's Phenomenon

**1990s Diet Advice:**
- Sugar industry secretly funded research
- Scientists blamed fat, not sugar
- "Low-fat" products actually high in sugar
- Obesity rates tripled during low-fat era

**The Problem:** When people gained weight eating low-fat foods, was it:
- Their metabolism (luck)?
- Their overall diet (other decisions)?
- The SnackWell's themselves (this decision)?

Working backward from outcomes to causes is hard when:
- Multiple factors contribute
- Hidden information exists
- Effects take time to manifest
- Confounding variables exist

### Uncertainty Amplifies Errors

**Rat Experiments:**
- Fixed reward schedule (pellet every 10 presses): Quick learning, quick extinction
- Variable reward schedule (pellet average every 10 presses): Slow extinction
- Uncertainty makes learning much harder

**Human Parallel:** Slot machines (variable rewards) packed with players despite being worst casino bets.

## Truth-Seeking Pods: The Buddy System

### Why Groups Matter

**Individual Limitations:**
- One viewpoint
- Own experiences only
- Blind to our own biases
- Limited perspective

**Group Benefits:**
- Multiple viewpoints
- Diverse experiences
- Spot each other's biases
- Fill in blind spots
- Share the work of de-biasing

> "You can observe a lot by watching." —Yogi Berra

But watching alone isn't enough—we need structured truth-seeking.

### The Charter: Three Core Elements

A truth-seeking pod needs an explicit charter addressing:

**1. Focus on Accuracy (over confirmation)**
- Reward truth-seeking, objectivity, open-mindedness
- Value calibrating beliefs over defending them
- Celebrate finding mistakes in winning outcomes
- Praise giving credit where due
- Make accuracy-seeking the path to approval

**2. Accountability**
- Advance notice that group will evaluate decisions
- Pre-commitment to loss limits, strategies, etc.
- Anticipate having to explain reasoning
- Reduces motivated reasoning in the moment

**3. Diversity of Viewpoints**
- Actively seek dissenting opinions
- Include people who initially opposed the decision
- Value hearing what can be said "by persons of every variety of opinion" (John Stuart Mill)

### Sample Charter Template

```
TRUTH-SEEKING POD CHARTER

Members: [Names]

Purpose: Improve decision-making by providing accountability and diverse perspectives

Meeting Schedule: [Frequency]

Core Commitments:

1. ACCURACY OVER DEFENSIVENESS
   - We celebrate finding mistakes in our reasoning
   - We give credit where credit is due (including to luck)
   - We express uncertainty appropriately
   - We reward "I was wrong" and "I'm not sure"

2. ACCOUNTABILITY
   - We commit to sharing major decisions before knowing outcomes when possible
   - We identify pre-commitments (loss limits, criteria, etc.)
   - We explain our reasoning process
   - We track predictions and calibrate beliefs

3. DIVERSITY OF THOUGHT
   - We actively seek dissenting opinions
   - We argue the other side to understand it
   - We apply uniform standards regardless of source
   - We assume positive intent in disagreement

4. CUDOS NORMS (see below)
   - Communism: Share all relevant information
   - Universalism: Evaluate ideas independent of source
   - Disinterestedness: Guard against conflicts of interest
   - Organized Skepticism: Encourage productive dissent

What Earns Approval in Our Group:
- Admitting mistakes and uncertainty
- Giving credit to others and to luck
- Finding alternative hypotheses
- Acknowledging when wrong
- Separating outcome quality from decision quality
- Updating beliefs based on evidence

What We Discourage:
- Results-oriented analysis
- Certainty without evidence
- Shooting the messenger
- Motivated reasoning
- Hiding uncomfortable details
```

### Group Approval as Reward

**The Motivation:** We crave approval, even from strangers we'll never meet again.

**The Strategy:** Reshape what earns approval:
- Not: "I'm so smart, look how I won"
- But: "I found three mistakes in my winning process"

**The Result:** New habits form when group rewards:
- Admitting mistakes
- Giving credit to others
- Finding alternative hypotheses
- Acknowledging uncertainty
- Separating outcome from decision quality

### The Minimum Viable Group

**Size:** Three people
- Two to disagree
- One to referee

Different friends serve different purposes—not everyone needs to be a truth-seeking buddy.

## CUDOS: Merton's Norms for Truth-Seeking Groups

Robert K. Merton (sociologist and former magician) established norms for scientific communities that apply to any truth-seeking group.

### C - Communism (Data Belongs to Group)

**Principle:** "More is more"

Share:
- All relevant details
- Information that makes you uncomfortable
- Context that might change interpretation
- What you don't know, not just what you do

**Why We Resist:**
- Fear of looking bad
- Desire to control narrative
- Self-enhancing bias

**The Fix:** Reward complete data sharing. When someone shares uncomfortable details, recognize it as strength, not weakness.

**Expert Example from Poker:**
Top poker players describe hands with exhaustive detail:
- Position of all players
- Bet sizes and pot sizes after each action
- What they know about opponents
- Recent hand history
- Stack sizes
- Any potentially relevant information

**Practice Exercise:**
When sharing a decision for feedback, deliberately include:
- One fact that makes you look bad
- One piece of missing information
- One assumption you're unsure about

### U - Universalism (Apply Uniform Standards)

**Principle:** "Don't shoot the message"

Evaluate ideas independent of source:
- Not: "That person's an idiot, so their idea is wrong"
- But: "What's the merit of this idea?"

**The Exercise:**
When you dismiss someone as "bad player" or "wrong about everything":
- Force yourself to find something they did well
- Imagine the idea coming from someone you respect
- Imagine your favorite idea coming from someone you don't respect

**Political Example:**
- Liberals: Read conservative sources looking for things to **agree** with
- Conservatives: Read liberal sources looking for things to **agree** with

This doesn't mean abandoning your position—it means understanding it better by understanding the other side.

**Practice Exercise:**
Take your Twitter follows or news sources. Can you find three things to genuinely agree with from sources you typically dismiss?

### D - Disinterestedness (Guard Against Conflicts)

**Principle:** We all have conflicts of interest, and they're contagious

**Types of Conflicts:**
- Financial (like sugar industry funding research)
- Outcome bias (knowing how it turned out)
- Belief confirmation (knowing what someone wants to hear)
- Ego protection (not wanting to be wrong)

**Strategy 1: Outcome Blindness**
Describe decisions without revealing outcomes:
- In poker: Describe hand up to decision point, omit result
- In business: Evaluate strategy before seeing sales figures
- In trials: Assess approach before verdict

**Example:**
"I'm second-and-goal at the 1-yard line with 26 seconds left, trailing by 4, with one timeout. I'm deciding between running and passing. Here's my analysis..."

(No mention of interception)

**Strategy 2: Belief Blindness**
Don't reveal your conclusion when seeking advice:
- Not: "I think we should fire him. What do you think?"
- But: "What should we do about this situation?" [describe situation]

**Strategy 3: Devil's Advocate Switching**
When two people disagree:
- Have each argue the other's position
- Winner = better debater, not defender of original position
- Moves interest from confirmation to understanding

**Practice Exercise:**
Before your next pod meeting, prepare a decision:
- Write out the situation without revealing the outcome
- Present without revealing your preferred choice
- Ask "What would you do?" not "Was I right?"

### S - Organized Skepticism (Encourage Dissent)

**Principle:** Skepticism is partnership, not antagonism

**Not:** "You're wrong!"

**But:**
- "I'm not sure about that"
- "Have you considered...?"
- "Are you sure about that?"
- "What would change your mind?"

**Institutionalizing Dissent Examples:**

**State Department Dissent Channel:**
- Employees can dissent without penalty
- Four annual awards for "constructive dissent"
- Led to policy changes (Bosnia, Syria immigration)

**CIA Red Teams:**
- Dedicated to arguing against conventional wisdom
- Spotting flaws in logic and analysis
- Used in bin Laden raid planning

**Corporate Applications:**
- Anonymous dissent channels
- Rotating devil's advocate role
- Premortem exercises (covered later)
- "Dissent bonus" in meetings

**Practice Exercise:**
In your next pod meeting, appoint one person as "designated skeptic" who must find three potential problems with each decision presented. Rotate the role.

## Temporal Discounting and Mental Time Travel

### The Night Jerry Problem

> "I stay up late at night because I'm Night Guy. Night Guy wants to stay up late. 'What about getting up after five hours of sleep?' 'That's Morning Guy's problem. That's not my problem. I'm Night Guy.'" —Jerry Seinfeld

**Temporal Discounting:** We favor present-self at expense of future-self

**Examples:**
- Staying up late (immediate fun vs. tomorrow's fatigue)
- Spending vs. saving (immediate gratification vs. retirement comfort)
- Eating junk food (immediate pleasure vs. long-term health)
- Breaking loss limits (immediate hope of recovery vs. bigger losses)

### The Brain Science

**Same Neural Network for:**
- Remembering the past
- Imagining the future

**Key Regions:**
- Hippocampus (memory)
- Prefrontal cortex (deliberative thinking)

**Insight:** "Thinking about the future is remembering the future"

We assemble memories in novel ways to imagine possible futures.

### Age Progression Technology

**The Retirement Problem:**
Present-self makes spending decisions; future-self lives with consequences.

**The Solution:**
- Show people photos of their aged future-self
- Face Retirement app (Bank of America)
- Virtual reality aged-self

**Stanford Study Results:**
- Seeing present-self: $73.90 allocated to retirement
- Seeing aged-self: $178.10 allocated to retirement
- **141% increase** from visualization

**Why It Works:** Brings future-us into present decision-making moment.

### 10-10-10: Suzy Welch's Framework

**Future Focus:**
"What are the consequences of each of my options in:
- Ten minutes?
- Ten months?
- Ten years?"

**Past Focus:**
"How would I feel today if I had made this decision:
- Ten minutes ago?
- Ten months ago?
- Ten years ago?"

**Purpose:**
- Triggers mental time travel
- Recruits deliberative mind
- Reduces temporal discounting
- Provides perspective on ticker-watching

**Practice Exercise:**
Take your next medium-sized decision and write out answers to all six questions. Notice how your perspective shifts.

### Regret: Moving It Forward in Time

**The Philosophers' Debate:**
- Nietzsche: Regret is "adding to the first act of stupidity a second"
- Thoreau: "Make the most of your regrets; never smother your sorrow"

**The Problem:** Regret occurs **after** decisions, when it's too late to change them

**The Solution:** Move regret **before** decisions

**Preemptive Regret Strategies:**

**1. Loss Limits (Poker Example):**
- Set $600 limit before playing
- When reached, imagine explaining to group why you kept playing
- Experience regret for breaking limit **before** breaking it
- Result: Easier to quit

**2. Age Progression:**
- See future-self struggling financially
- Experience regret for not saving **before** spending
- Motivates better allocation

**3. Imagined Accountability:**
- Know you'll answer to truth-seeking group
- Run that conversation in advance
- Feel disapproval for poor choice before making it

**Benefits:**
- **Immediate:** Better decisions in the moment
- **Long-term:** Less consuming regret after outcomes
- **Emotional:** Self-compassion from having prepared for negative outcomes

## Ticker Watching and the Zoom Lens

### The Magnification Problem

**Example: Flat Tire in Rain**
- In the moment: "Worst day ever"
- One year later: Funny cocktail party story

**The Pattern:** We magnify present moments out of proportion to their actual impact on long-term happiness.

### Stock Market Parallel

**Berkshire Hathaway Long View:**
Steady upward trajectory over decades.

**Berkshire Hathaway Daily:**
Frightening ups and downs that mean nothing in context.

**Banking Crisis (2008-2009):**
Looked catastrophic day-to-day, but irrelevant to long-term trajectory.

### Path Dependence

**Scenario 1:**
- Win $1,000 in 30 minutes
- Lose it back over next 90 minutes
- End: Break even
- Feeling: Terrible

**Scenario 2:**
- Lose $1,000 in 30 minutes
- Win it back over next 90 minutes
- End: Break even
- Feeling: Ecstatic

**More Scenarios:**
- Up $1,000, then down to +$100: Sad about **winning** $100
- Down $1,000, then up to -$100: Happy about **losing** $100

**The Insight:**
> "What has happened in the recent past drives our emotional response much more than how we are doing overall."

### The Distortion

The zoom lens doesn't just magnify—it **distorts** based on recent changes, not absolute position.

**Time-Travel Solution:**
Ask: "How would I feel about this a year from now?"

**Practice Exercise:**
Keep a "perspective journal":
- Record "worst day" / "best day" moments
- Revisit 1 month, 3 months, 6 months later
- Rate actual impact vs. felt impact at the time
- Build calibration for zoom lens distortion

## Tilt: When Emotions Override Decisions

### Definition

"Bad outcomes can have an impact on your emotions that compromise your decision-making going forward so that you make emotionally charged, irrational decisions that are likely to result in more bad outcomes that will then negatively impact your decision-making going forward and so on."

**Origin:** Pinball machines that shut down when shaken—"TILT" flashes on display

### The Brain Science

**What Happens:**
- Amygdala (emotional center) activates
- Shuts down prefrontal cortex
- Deliberative thinking becomes impossible

**Physical Signs:**
- Flushed cheeks
- Racing heart
- Rapid breathing
- Raised voice
- Tense muscles

**Verbal Cues:**
- "Seriously? Again?"
- "I don't know why I bother"
- "Why does this always happen to me?"
- "This is unbelievable"
- "I can't believe this"

### Beyond Poker

**Tilt Occurs In:**
- Arguments with partners
- Bad service at restaurants
- Workplace conflicts
- Canceled sales
- Dismissed ideas
- Traffic situations
- Sports (both playing and watching)
- Social media arguments

### Intervention Strategies

**1. Recognition:**
Notice verbal and physiological signs. Create a personal tilt checklist.

**2. Precommitment:**
Decide in advance:
- "When I feel X, I will do Y"
- Walk away from situation
- Take 10 deep breaths
- Sleep on it
- Call truth-seeking buddy

**3. 10-10-10 Questions:**
- "What's happened when I've felt this way before?"
- "Will this help me make better decisions?"
- "How will this matter in 10 months?"

**4. Group Support:**
- Charter includes tilt-detection
- "Do you think you're on tilt?"
- "Is this really going to matter long-term?"
- Positive reinforcement for recognizing and avoiding tilt

### The Long View

> "It's all just one long poker game."

Aphorism reminds us: This moment is one uptick/downtick in a much longer trajectory.

**Practice Exercise:**
Create a "Tilt Response Card" to carry:

```
TILT WARNING SIGNS:
[Your personal signs]

WHEN TILTED, I WILL:
1. [Pre-committed action]
2. [Pre-committed action]
3. [Pre-committed action]

LONG VIEW REMINDER:
[Personal phrase that helps you zoom out]
```

## Ulysses Contracts: Precommitment Strategies

### The Original Ulysses Contract

Odysseus knew he would be entranced by Sirens' song:
- Had crew fill their ears with wax
- Had them tie his hands to mast
- Past-self binding future-self to better behavior

### Modern Applications

**Barrier-Inducing Contracts:**
- Use ride-sharing when going to bar (removes car keys)
- Automatic retirement allocation from paycheck
- Throwing out junk food
- Loss limits with poker group
- No bread basket at restaurant
- Time limits (6-8 hour poker sessions)
- Phone in different room at night

**Barrier-Reducing Contracts:**
- Carry healthy snacks (easier to make good choice)
- Prepare workout clothes night before
- Put alarm clock across room
- Subscription to gym near work
- Pre-scheduled meetings (harder to cancel)

### The Spectrum of Binding

**Most Binding:**
Physical prevention (no car keys, automatic deductions)

**Medium Binding:**
Requires effort to undo (must order bread, must go buy junk food)

**Least Binding:**
Simple commitment (still powerful with accountability)

### Investment Applications

**With Financial Advisor:**
- Determine in advance conditions for buying/selling
- If emotional decision arises, advisor reminds of agreement
- Creates decision-interrupt

**With Yourself:**
- Set rules for portfolio allocation
- Define triggers for rebalancing
- Establish loss limits or gain-taking rules
- Write them down and commit to review before any trades

### The Decision Swear Jar

**Concept:** Identify language/thoughts that signal irrational thinking

**Warning Signs to Watch:**

**Certainty Illusions:**
- "I know," "I'm sure," "I knew it"
- "Always," "never," "100%," "0%"
- "Best," "worst," "everyone," "no one"

**Irrational Outcome Fielding:**
- "I can't believe how unlucky I got"
- "I planned it perfectly"
- "They totally had that coming"

**Shooting the Messenger:**
- "Idiot," "typical [group]"
- "Gun nut," "bleeding heart"
- Ad hominem dismissals

**Zoom Lens:**
- "Worst day ever"
- "Day from hell"
- "This is the end"

**Motivated Reasoning:**
- "Conventional wisdom"
- "Everyone agrees"
- "Can you prove it's not true?"
- "That's just obvious"

**Lack of Self-Compassion:**
- "I should have known"
- "How could I be so stupid?"
- "I'm such an idiot"

**Conflict Creation:**
- Leading with conclusion when asking advice
- Revealing outcome before seeking input

**Closing to Input:**
- Starting with "No" or "But"
- Not using "Yes, and..."

**Purpose:** Each phrase triggers decision-interrupt, reminder to engage deliberative mind.

**Practice Exercise:**
Choose 3-5 phrases from above that you use most. Track them for a week. Each time you catch yourself, pause and engage 10-10-10 thinking.

## Reconnaissance: Scenario Planning

### Military Parallel

**Operation Overlord (D-Day):**
- Largest seaborne invasion in history
- Countless things could go wrong
- Many things **did** go wrong
- Succeeded because they prepared for multiple scenarios

**Bin Laden Raid:**
- Extensive reconnaissance
- Multiple scenario plans
- "What if" for various conditions
- Precommitments for different outcomes

### The Process

**Step 1: Identify Possible Futures**

```
Decision
   ↓
├─ Outcome A (Probability X%)
├─ Outcome B (Probability Y%)
├─ Outcome C (Probability Z%)
└─ Outcome D (Probability W%)

Where X% + Y% + Z% + W% = 100%
```

**Step 2: Estimate Probabilities**

Don't let perfect be enemy of good:
- "I can't know exact probability" → You're already guessing implicitly!
- Wide range (20-80%) better than no estimate
- Probability of scenarios must sum to 100%
- Forces explicit recognition of uncertainty

**Step 3: Go Deeper**

For each outcome, consider:
- How we'll respond
- What decisions follow
- What scenarios emerge from those
- Multi-level decision trees

Expert poker players think multiple decisions ahead, considering opponents' responses to each action.

### Benefits

**1. Realistic View:**
Explicitly acknowledges uncertainty.

**2. Better Preparation:**
Ready for multiple futures, not surprised.

**3. Reduced Regret/Euphoria:**
Expected various outcomes, not shocked when one occurs.

**4. Protects Against Hindsight Bias:**
Memorialized all possible futures, not just the one that happened.

**5. Enables Nimble Response:**
Planned contingencies, can pivot quickly.

### Nate Silver's Approach

> "When faced with highly uncertain conditions, military units and major corporations sometimes use an exercise called scenario planning. The idea is to consider a broad range of possibilities for how the future might unfold to help guide long-term planning and preparation."

**Practice Exercise:**
Take an upcoming decision. Create a scenario tree:
- List 4-6 possible outcomes
- Assign probabilities (must sum to 100%)
- For each, identify your response
- Document before outcome occurs
- Review after to calibrate

## Backcasting: Working Backward from Success

### The Concept

**Traditional planning:** Stand at beginning, look forward
**Backcasting:** Stand at goal achieved, look backward

**Why It's Better:**
- From present: Next step looms large, everything beyond is blurry
- From future: Entire path is visible with equal clarity
- Removes distortion of present perspective

### Research Support

**Harvard Study (Mitchell, Russo, Pennington, 1989):**
> "Prospective hindsight—imagining that an event has already occurred—increases the ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes by 30%."

### The Process

**Step 1: Imagine the Future**
Hold up newspaper with headline: "We Achieved Our Goal!"
- Make it specific: date, details, metrics
- Visualize concretely: who's celebrating, what changed
- Feel the success as if it already happened

**Step 2: Work Backward**
From that successful future, look back:
- What events occurred that led here?
- What decisions were made?
- What went our way?
- What obstacles did we overcome?
- What strategies proved effective?
- What was the sequence of milestones?

**Step 3: Identify Necessary Steps**
Map path from future back to present:
- Which events were most critical?
- Which were necessary vs. helpful?
- What had to happen first?
- What dependencies existed?

**Step 4: Plan Implementation**
Based on backward view:
- What should we do first?
- What can we control vs. what requires luck?
- Where should we invest resources?
- What's most likely to fail?

### Examples

**Enterprise (Double Market Share):**
- Headline: "Company X Has Doubled Its Market Share by December 2026"
- Work backward: New product launched successfully, won three major clients, competitor stumbled, hired key talent, marketing campaign resonated
- Identify: Which had to happen? Which sequence?
- Adjust: Are probabilities realistic? If not, revise goal or increase probability of key events

**Trial Lawyer:**
- Headline: "Victory at Trial: Jury Awards Full Damages"
- What favorable rulings occurred?
- What testimony resonated?
- What evidence was key?
- How did jury respond throughout?
- What objections were successful?

**Personal (Weight Loss):**
- Headline: "Lost 20 Pounds in 6 Months - Feeling Great!"
- How did we avoid junk food?
- What exercise became routine?
- How did we stick to plan?
- What obstacles came up and how did we overcome them?
- What support system helped?

### Historical Example

**Frederick Law Olmsted and Central Park:**
- Opened 1858, construction finished 1873
- Visitors saw barren land and small plantings
- Olmsted saw mature park decades in future
- Designed for what it would become, not what it was
- Planted trees that would be perfect in 50 years

## Premortems: Working Backward from Failure

### The Concept

Backcasting visualizes success; premortem visualizes failure.

**Both Are Necessary:**
- Backcasting reveals positive space
- Premortem reveals negative space
- Together: Complete picture

### The Process

**Step 1: Imagine Failure**
Hold up newspaper: "We Failed to Reach Our Goal"
- Make it specific: date, how badly failed, consequences
- Visualize concretely: who's affected, what went wrong
- Feel the disappointment as if it already happened

**Step 2: Work Backward**
From that failed future, look back:
- Why did we fail?
- What went wrong?
- What obstacles emerged?
- What assumptions were wrong?
- What did we overlook?
- Where was the plan flawed?

**Step 3: Identify Risks**
Categorize failure causes:
- Things we can control
- Things we can influence
- Things outside our control
- Early warning signs for each

**Step 4: Plan Mitigation**
For each identified risk:
- Can we prevent it? How?
- Can we detect it early? What signals?
- Can we prepare contingency? What response?
- Should we adjust goals? How?

### Why It Works

**Organized Skepticism:**
- Premortem changes game rules
- Being "team player" means being productive heckler
- Competing to find best reasons for failure
- Dissent is encouraged, not punished

**Permission for Dissent:**
- "We failed. Why?" frees people to identify problems
- Not saying plan is wrong, just identifying risks
- Reservations are heard without being labeled naysayers
- Reduces resentment if things don't work out

**Better Planning:**
- Anticipate obstacles
- Prepare responses
- Enable nimble adjustments
- Reduce surprise
- Build contingency plans

### Research: Negative Visualization Works

**Gabriele Oettingen (20+ years research):**

**Weight Loss Study:**
- Positive fantasizers: Lost 24 pounds **less**
- Those who imagined obstacles: More successful

**Pattern Across Contexts:**
- College students with unrequited crushes
- Job seekers
- Students before midterms
- Hip replacement patients

**Conclusion:** Positive goals + negative visualization = success

Pure positive thinking actually undermines achievement by making brain believe goal is already accomplished.

### Examples

**Enterprise (Market Share Goal):**
- Headline: "Company X Fails to Reach Market Share Goal - Analysis"
- Reasons: New product delayed 8 months, lost key VP of Engineering, competitor released superior product, economic downturn reduced overall market, failed to pivot when paradigm shifted, misread customer needs

**Trial Lawyer:**
- Key evidence ruled inadmissible
- Undiscovered evidence surfaces during trial
- Unsympathetic judge biased against our position
- Jury dislikes key witnesses
- Expert witnesses unexpectedly cancelled
- Unexpected expert testimony from other side
- Our timeline proved incorrect

**Personal (Weight Loss):**
- Birthday cakes at work constantly
- Bagels in every conference room
- Hard to find gym time with work travel
- Easy to procrastinate when tired
- Social pressure to eat poorly at events
- Winter makes exercise harder
- Stress triggers emotional eating

### The Balance

**Backcasting alone:**
- Overrepresents positive space
- Feeds natural optimism
- Underestimates risks

**With Premortem:**
- Positive and negative must sum to 100%
- Seeing large negative space shrinks positive space
- More accurate representation of uncertainty
- Better calibrated expectations

**Practical Tip:** Do premortem first. Easier to be skeptical before getting emotionally attached to backcasting vision.

## Hindsight Bias: The Chainsaw Problem

### The Tree Metaphor

**Structure:**
- Trunk = The Past (singular)
- Branches = Possible Futures (multiple)
- Thick branches = More probable futures
- Thin branches = Less probable futures
- Present = Where trunk meets branches

**The Problem:**
When one future becomes reality, we chainsaw off all other branches and act like it was inevitable.

### Examples Revisited

**Grain Bin Explosion (Jentz v. ConAgra):**
- Foreman sent workers into tunnel to clear tools
- Bin exploded, workers severely injured
- Jury awarded $180 million
- Judge Easterbrook: "Appears to be consequence of hindsight bias"
- Once explosion occurred, seemed inevitable
- But at the time, explosion was one of many possible futures

**Pete Carroll's Pass:**
- 2-3% chance of interception
- Once it happened, seemed like terrible call
- All other futures (touchdown, incomplete pass, sack, etc.) forgotten
- 98% of branches chainsawed away

**Steve Bartman:**
- Tiny probability he'd touch ball
- Even tinier probability Cubs would lose after that
- Required multiple subsequent unlikely events
- Once it happened, seemed inevitable, all his fault

### The CEO's Hindsight

Fired president → bad outcomes → "worst decision ever"

**Reality:**
- Thoughtful process
- Multiple alternatives considered
- Executive coaching attempted
- Reasonable belief they'd find better candidate

**After reconstruction:**
All the branches (ways it could have gone well) reattached. Decision quality was actually good; outcome was unlucky.

### Prevention Strategy

**Memorialize Scenario Plans:**
- Document all possible futures considered
- Record probability estimates
- Keep decision trees
- Refer back after outcomes occur

**Purpose:**
- Remember how uncertain it was
- Recall all the branches
- Resist hindsight bias
- Enable better calibration
- Reduce regret and euphoria

### 2016 Election Example

**Polls:** Clinton 60-70%, Trump 30-40%

**After Trump Won:**
- "Pollsters were wrong"
- "They missed it completely"
- "How could they be so blind?"

**Reality:**
- 30-40% isn't 0%
- Trump branch was substantial, not tiny
- Clinton branch severed, treated as if it never existed
- Pollsters were reasonably well-calibrated

> "We're all going to experience plenty of unfavorable outcomes. We can always, however, make a good bet. And even when we make a bad bet, we usually get a second chance because we can learn from the experience."

## Calibration Practice

### What Is Calibration?

Calibration = matching confidence to actual outcomes

**Well-Calibrated:**
- Things you say are 90% likely happen ~90% of the time
- Things you say are 30% likely happen ~30% of the time

**Poorly-Calibrated:**
- Overconfident: Things you say are 90% likely happen 60% of the time
- Underconfident: Things you say are 30% likely happen 60% of the time

### Why It Matters

Better calibration = better decisions:
- More accurate risk assessment
- Better resource allocation
- Appropriate contingency planning
- Less regret and surprise

### Calibration Tracking

**Basic Method:**

1. Make predictions with probabilities
2. Record them before outcomes known
3. Track outcomes
4. Calculate accuracy over time

**Example:**
- Prediction: "70% chance this grant comes through"
- Outcome: Rejected
- Log: One data point in your 70% bucket
- Over time: Do 70% of your "70% predictions" come true?

### Calibration Categories to Track

**Personal:**
- Time estimates ("This will take 2 hours" - 80% confidence)
- Social predictions ("She'll say yes" - 60% confidence)
- Health/fitness ("I'll lose 2 pounds this week" - 70% confidence)

**Professional:**
- Project completion ("Ship by Friday" - 85% confidence)
- Sales forecasts ("50% chance we close this deal")
- Hiring decisions ("90% chance she'll be a strong performer")

**Truth-Seeking Pod:**
- Decision outcomes ("70% chance this strategy works")
- Market predictions ("60% chance of rate cut")
- Event forecasts ("80% chance product launches Q1")

### Improving Calibration

**Reference Classes:**
Use base rates from similar situations:
- "Projects like this take 6-8 weeks"
- "Deals this size close 40% of the time"
- Not: "I feel confident" but: "The data suggests X%"

**Red Teams:**
Have someone argue why you're wrong
- Forces consideration of alternatives
- Reveals blind spots
- Typically moves confidence toward 50%

**Post-Mortems:**
After outcomes, analyze:
- What did I get right?
- What did I miss?
- What would have changed my probability?
- Apply lessons to next estimate

**Practice Exercises:**

**Exercise 1: Trivia Calibration**
Answer trivia questions with confidence levels:
- What year did Elvis die? [Your answer] - [Confidence %]
- Track accuracy across confidence levels
- Reveals over/underconfidence patterns

**Exercise 2: Weather Forecasting**
Predict tomorrow's weather with probability:
- "60% chance of rain"
- Track over months
- Compare to actual weather
- Trains probabilistic thinking

**Exercise 3: Project Estimation**
Estimate task completion times:
- Give 50%, 70%, and 90% confidence ranges
- Track actual completion times
- Adjust ranges based on historical accuracy

## Case Study: After-School All-Stars (ASAS)

### The Problem

National nonprofit providing after-school programs for 70,000 youth.

**Grant Application Challenge:**
- Heavy dependence on grant funding
- Uncertainty in award process
- Difficult budget planning
- Misunderstanding of "worth"

**Initial Approach:**
- Listed all outstanding grants
- Column for award amounts
- Treated award amount as "worth"
- Result: "The $200K grant is worth more than the $50K grant"

### The Shift: Expected Value

**Expected Value Formula:**
Expected Value = Award Amount × Probability of Getting Grant

**Examples:**
- $100,000 grant, 25% chance = **$25,000 expected value**
- $200,000 grant, 10% chance = **$20,000 expected value**
- $50,000 grant, 70% chance = **$35,000 expected value**

**Counterintuitive Finding:**
The $50,000 grant was worth MORE than the $200,000 grant.

### Implementation Benefits

**1. Better Work Stack:**
- Previously: Prioritized by award amount (put senior staff on $200K grant)
- Now: Prioritized by expected value (put senior staff on $50K grant with 70% chance)
- More efficient resource allocation
- Higher total expected revenue

**2. Realistic Budgeting:**
- Greater confidence in revenue projections
- Based on expected value of grant portfolio
- Better planning for various scenarios
- Can calculate 90%, 50%, and 10% funding scenarios

**3. Improved Calibration:**
- Following up after rejections (already doing)
- Following up after acceptances (new!)
- Focus on understanding what worked
- Questions: What made this 70% vs 25%? What can we replicate?
- Better probability estimates over time
- Higher quality applications

**4. Strategic Action:**
- Identified ways to increase win probability
- Example: Personal site visits increased probability 15 percentage points
- Committed to doing more site visits for high-value grants
- Measured effectiveness of changes

**5. Bias Protection:**
- Less hindsight bias (anticipated both outcomes)
- Less resulting (evaluated process in advance)
- When $200K grant didn't come through: "We expected that 90% of the time, and that's what happened"
- When $50K grant came through: "We expected that 70% of the time, so this was likely"

**6. Cultural Expansion:**
- Success led to broader implementation
- Scenario planning across departments
- Part of organizational decision-making culture
- Board meetings now include expected value discussions

### Broader Application

**Sales Prospecting:**
Same process applies to any sales organization:
- Assign probabilities to closing each deal
- Calculate expected value of sales pipeline
- Prioritize by expected value, not deal size
- Budget based on expected revenue
- Evaluate and calibrate predictions
- Protect against resulting and hindsight bias

## Common Pitfalls in Practice

### Pitfall 1: Resulting Without Realizing It

**What it looks like:**
- "That was a great call" (after win)
- "We should have done X" (after loss)
- Judging past decisions with present knowledge

**Why it happens:**
- Outcome creates hindsight bias
- Emotional response overrides analysis
- Natural tendency to chainsaw branches

**How to avoid:**
- Always review scenario plan first (what did we know then?)
- Ask: "Would I judge this differently if outcome flipped?"
- Separate process quality from outcome luck
- Share with pod outcome-blind when possible

**Real-world example:**
CEO fires president → company struggles → board criticizes decision

**Better approach:**
"Given what we knew then, the process was sound. The board considered alternatives, tried coaching, had reason to believe we'd find a better fit. The 40% bad outcome happened. What can we learn from execution?"

### Pitfall 2: Expressing False Precision

**What it looks like:**
- "I'm 73.6% confident"
- Probabilities to multiple decimal places
- Treating estimates as exact measurements

**Why it happens:**
- Misunderstanding what probabilities represent
- Trying to appear rigorous
- Not recognizing uncertainty in uncertainty

**How to avoid:**
- Use round numbers (60%, not 63%)
- Use ranges when appropriate (40-60%)
- Acknowledge: "Rough estimate, but better than not estimating"
- Focus on calibration over time, not single-event precision

**Real-world example:**
"Our model shows 73.247% probability of success"

**Better approach:**
"We estimate roughly 70% chance of success, give or take 10 percentage points. We'll track outcomes to improve calibration."

### Pitfall 3: Premortems That Become Negative Group-Think

**What it looks like:**
- Entire group becomes pessimistic
- Focus only on negative scenarios
- Morale drops, initiative dies
- "Why even try?" mentality

**Why it happens:**
- Premortem without backcasting balance
- Permission for negativity becomes culture of negativity
- Loss aversion magnifies perceived risks

**How to avoid:**
- Always pair premortem with backcasting
- Remember: They must sum to 100%
- Treat premortem as risk mitigation, not defeatism
- Charter emphasizes accuracy, not pessimism
- Celebrate finding risks early (when can address them)

**Real-world example:**
Team does premortem, lists 47 ways project could fail, concludes it's hopeless.

**Better approach:**
"We've identified 15 significant risks. We can mitigate 8 directly, prepare contingencies for 5, and 2 are unlikely enough to accept. Given this risk mitigation, our probability of success is 65%, up from 40% without planning."

### Pitfall 4: Truth-Seeking Pod Becomes Echo Chamber

**What it looks like:**
- Everyone agrees all the time
- No real skepticism
- Diverse viewpoints not actually diverse
- Group reinforces existing biases

**Why it happens:**
- Selected members who think similarly
- Confuses "friendly" with "truth-seeking"
- Shooting messenger still happening subtly
- Lack of universalism in practice

**How to avoid:**
- Actively recruit different perspectives
- Rotate designated skeptic role
- Include people who opposed decisions
- Track: Are we changing minds? Updating beliefs?
- Test: Would outsider see real disagreement?

**Real-world example:**
Poker pod of aggressive players who all think tight players are "wrong."

**Better approach:**
Include one conservative player, force aggressive players to articulate why their approach works in specific situations vs. generally, find situations where tight play is correct.

### Pitfall 5: Temporal Discounting Still Wins

**What it looks like:**
- Know 10-10-10 but don't use it
- Saw age progression but still don't save
- Understand Night Jerry but still stay up late
- Gap between knowing and doing

**Why it happens:**
- Present-self has executive control
- Future benefits are abstract
- Immediate gratification is concrete
- Missing accountability

**How to avoid:**
- Ulysses contracts (binding commitments)
- Truth-seeking pod accountability (report back)
- Make future consequences present (age progression)
- Precommit publicly to pod
- Smaller commitments more frequently (not "save for retirement" but "save $100 this week")

**Real-world example:**
"I know I should save for retirement, but this vacation is right now."

**Better approach:**
- Show aged-self image on phone
- Pre-commit to truth-seeking pod: "I will save $500/month, you'll ask me about it"
- Automatic deduction so future-self choice is default
- Reward present-self differently (non-financial treat)

### Pitfall 6: Tilt Goes Unrecognized

**What it looks like:**
- Making emotional decisions
- "I'm fine!" (while clearly not fine)
- Escalating commitment to losing path
- Revenge trading/deciding

**Why it happens:**
- Amygdala shuts down prefrontal cortex
- Can't think rationally while tilted
- Pride prevents admitting emotional state
- "I should be able to handle this"

**How to avoid:**
- External tilt-checkers (pod members can see it)
- Physical signals checklist (heart rate, flushed face, raised voice)
- Precommitted circuit breakers ("I walk away when X")
- Practice: Actually walk away in low-stakes situations
- Celebrate tilt recognition (not weakness, but strength)

**Real-world example:**
CEO loses key client, immediately wants to fire sales team.

**Better approach:**
- Pod member: "Are you on tilt? Your voice is raised and you're using absolutes."
- CEO: "You're right. I'm going to sleep on this."
- Next day: Analyzes what was luck vs. skill, makes considered decision

### Pitfall 7: Shooting the Messenger Despite Knowing Better

**What it looks like:**
- "Yeah, but he's an idiot so..."
- Dismissing ideas based on source
- Ad hominem in disguise
- "I know I shouldn't do this, but..."

**Why it happens:**
- Unconscious bias is strong
- Easy to rationalize ("This case is different")
- Ego wants to be right
- Group status matters

**How to avoid:**
- Force yourself to find one thing to agree with
- Imagine idea from different source
- Devil's advocate switching (argue their position)
- Pod enforces universalism actively
- Track: Are we dismissing people consistently?

**Real-world example:**
"That's the same guy who said Y would happen and it didn't, so we can ignore his input on X."

**Better approach:**
"Let's evaluate the idea independent of source. Imagine our favorite advisor said this—what's the merit? He was wrong about Y for reasons A, B, C. Do those reasons apply here?"

### Pitfall 8: Scenario Planning Becomes Box-Checking

**What it looks like:**
- "We did a premortem" (10 minutes, superficial)
- Going through motions without thinking
- Not actually using scenarios to prepare
- Filed away and forgotten

**Why it happens:**
- Treating as compliance, not insight
- No accountability for using scenarios
- Time pressure
- Not seeing value from previous scenarios

**How to avoid:**
- Build scenarios before committed to decision
- Actually prepare contingencies for each scenario
- Review scenarios after outcomes
- Track: Did scenarios help? What did we miss?
- Make it part of major decision process, not optional

**Real-world example:**
Team does quick premortem before launch, identifies 5 risks, does nothing about them, surprised when 2 occur.

**Better approach:**
Detailed premortem identifies 15 risks. Create mitigation plan for top 10. Build early warning systems for top 5. Assign owners. Review weekly. When risk #3 starts showing early warnings, activate contingency plan.

### Pitfall 9: Certainty Creeps Back In

**What it looks like:**
- Starting with probabilities, ending with certainty
- "I'm 80% sure... well actually I'm certain"
- Social pressure toward appearing confident
- Forgetting to express uncertainty

**Why it happens:**
- Cultural conditioning toward certainty
- Uncertainty feels weak
- Others reward certainty
- Decision-makers want confidence

**How to avoid:**
- Decision swear jar (catch "I know" language)
- Pod rewards uncertainty expression
- Track: Are we expressing more or less uncertainty over time?
- Remember: Thoughtful uncertainty > false certainty

**Real-world example:**
"I think this has 60% chance of working... but we should definitely do it... I'm sure it will work."

**Better approach:**
"I estimate 60% chance this works. That's good enough to move forward, and we've prepared contingencies for the 40% downside. I'm comfortable with that bet given the potential upside."

### Pitfall 10: Forgetting "It's All One Long Game"

**What it looks like:**
- Ticker watching individual outcomes
- Each decision treated as isolated
- Win/loss accounting for single events
- Losing perspective on trajectory

**Why it happens:**
- Path dependence affects emotions
- Recent changes loom large
- Natural zoom lens on present
- Loss aversion magnifies downturns

**How to avoid:**
- Regular long-view check-ins
- Track trajectory, not individual points
- 10-10-10 backward questions
- Pod reminds: "How will this matter in a year?"
- Visual: Graph your "happiness stock" not "happiness daily"

**Real-world example:**
Lost $500 in poker session → "Worst night ever, I'm terrible at this"

**Better approach:**
"I'm down $500 tonight, which moves my 6-month total from +$3,000 to +$2,500. My decisions were solid in 7 of 8 key hands. The one mistake cost about $100. I'm playing well overall, and this is normal variance in one long game."

## Practice Exercises

### Exercise 1: Resulting Detective

**Setup:** For one week, track every time you observe resulting (in yourself or others).

**Categories:**
- Sports commentary
- Business news
- Personal life
- Social media
- Workplace

**Log:**
| Instance | What happened | Resulting statement | Better statement |
|----------|---------------|---------------------|------------------|
| | | | |

**Reflection:**
- Where do you see resulting most?
- What patterns emerge?
- How would thinking in bets change the narrative?

### Exercise 2: Confidence Calibration (Trivia)

**Setup:** Answer 20 trivia questions with confidence levels.

**Format:**
1. Question: [Question]
2. Your answer: [Answer]
3. Confidence: [%]

**After completing all 20:**
- Look up correct answers
- Calculate accuracy by confidence bucket
- Things you marked 90%: Did 90% of those answers turn out correct?
- Things you marked 70%: Did 70% correct?
- Things you marked 50%: Did 50% correct?

**Reflection:**
- Are you overconfident? (Reality < Confidence)
- Underconfident? (Reality > Confidence)
- Well-calibrated? (Reality ≈ Confidence)

### Exercise 3: Pete Carroll Redux

**Setup:** Find a recent controversial decision in sports, business, or politics.

**Questions:**
1. What was the decision?
2. What was the outcome?
3. What is the prevailing narrative about whether it was a "good" or "bad" decision?
4. Now do the analysis:
   - What were the possible futures at decision time?
   - What were the probabilities of each?
   - What was the quality of the decision process?
   - Was outcome luck or skill?
5. Write the "defense of the decision" focusing on process quality
6. Write the "criticism of the decision" focusing on process flaws (not outcome)

**Reflection:**
How different is your analysis from the prevailing narrative?

### Exercise 4: Scenario Planning Practice

**Setup:** Choose an upcoming decision in your life.

**Steps:**
1. Write decision clearly
2. Identify 4-6 possible outcomes
3. Assign probabilities (must sum to 100%)
4. For each outcome, write:
   - What happens
   - How likely
   - What you'll do in response
5. Document this BEFORE decision occurs
6. After outcome, review:
   - Which scenario occurred?
   - Were you prepared?
   - How accurate were probabilities?
   - What did you learn?

### Exercise 5: Backcasting Personal Goal

**Setup:** Choose a 6-month personal goal.

**Steps:**
1. Write specific goal
2. Imagine it's 6 months from now, you achieved it
3. Write newspaper headline
4. Describe successful future (3-5 sentences)
5. Work backward: What happened? (10-15 events/decisions)
6. Identify critical path
7. What should you do first?

**Implementation:**
- Create action plan based on backward view
- Set 3 immediate next actions
- Schedule 1-month check-in
- Track: Is backward view helping?

### Exercise 6: Premortem Challenge

**Setup:** Take same goal from Exercise 5.

**Steps:**
1. Imagine it's 6 months from now, you FAILED
2. Write newspaper headline about failure
3. Work backward: Why did you fail? (15-20 reasons)
4. Categorize:
   - Can control (mitigate directly)
   - Can influence (increase success probability)
   - Cannot control (prepare contingency)
5. For top 10 risks, create mitigation plan
6. Identify early warning signs

**Reflection:**
- How did premortem change your perspective?
- What risks would you have missed?
- How does it balance with backcasting?

### Exercise 7: Outcome Fielding Deep Dive

**Setup:** Choose one recent success and one recent failure.

**For Success:**
1. What happened?
2. Initial reaction: Why did you succeed?
3. Now challenge: What was luck?
4. What was skill?
5. What could you have done better?
6. What should you NOT repeat (lucky behaviors)?

**For Failure:**
1. What happened?
2. Initial reaction: Why did you fail?
3. Now challenge: What was luck?
4. What was skill?
5. What did you do well?
6. What should you repeat (good process that got unlucky)?

**Reflection:**
How different is challenged version from initial reaction?

### Exercise 8: Build Your Decision Swear Jar

**Setup:** Create personal list of phrases that signal bias.

**Categories:**
- Certainty illusions ("I know," "always," "never")
- Outcome fielding ("I can't believe how unlucky")
- Shooting messenger ("Idiot")
- Zoom lens ("Worst day ever")
- Motivated reasoning ("Everyone agrees")
- Self-flagellation ("I should have known")

**Implementation:**
1. Choose 5 phrases you use most
2. Track for one week
3. Each time you catch yourself:
   - Pause
   - Rephrase with uncertainty
   - Apply 10-10-10
4. Report to pod

**Reflection:**
Which biases are hardest for you to catch?

### Exercise 9: Ulysses Contract Design

**Setup:** Identify one area where you consistently make poor decisions due to temporal discounting.

**Design Process:**
1. What's the pattern? (Present-self wanting X, hurting future-self)
2. What's the trigger? (When does this happen?)
3. What barrier can you create?
   - Physical (remove temptation)
   - Effortful (add steps)
   - Accountability (commit to someone)
4. Create specific contract
5. Identify enforcement mechanism
6. Start date and review date

**Implementation:**
- Write contract explicitly
- Share with pod
- Track adherence
- Review: What worked? What didn't?

### Exercise 10: Truth-Seeking Pod Role Play

**Setup:** Practice CUDOS norms before forming real pod.

**Roles:**
- Person A: Presents decision (with common biases)
- Person B: Practices each CUDOS norm
- Observer: Notes what works/doesn't

**Scenarios:**
1. **Communism violation:** A shares decision but omits uncomfortable details
   - B: How to elicit complete information?

2. **Universalism violation:** A dismisses idea because of source
   - B: How to focus on idea merit?

3. **Disinterestedness violation:** A reveals outcome before describing decision
   - B: How to create outcome blindness?

4. **Skepticism violation:** A is defensive about decision
   - B: How to question productively?

**Reflection:**
Which CUDOS norm is hardest to practice?

## Advanced Integration

### Thinking in Bets for Different Contexts

**For Entrepreneurs/Startups:**

Decision: Pivot vs. persevere
- Scenario plan: Success if pivot (30%), success if persevere (20%), failure both ways (50%)
- Backcast: Pivot succeeded - why? Customer needs were different than assumed, new market more responsive
- Premortem: Pivot failed - why? Lost existing customers, new market didn't materialize, team not aligned
- Expected value: Pivot EV = $X million × 30%, Persevere EV = $Y million × 20%
- Ulysses contract: Commit to decision, stop waffling after 90 days
- Truth-seeking pod: Include someone who opposes pivot, someone who opposes persevere, neutral party

**For Leaders/Managers:**

Decision: Hire candidate A vs. B vs. keep searching
- Scenario plan: A succeeds (60%), B succeeds (70%), neither works (40% for A, 30% for B), great candidate found if keep searching (30%, but takes 3 months)
- Backcast: A succeeded - why? Skills matched actual needs, culture fit good, ramp time acceptable
- Premortem: A failed - why? Overestimated skills in interview, culture clash emerged, took too long to ramp
- Outcome fielding: If A fails, what was luck vs. hiring process? If succeeds, what was luck vs. good interview?
- Truth-seeking pod: Include someone who interviewed candidate, someone who didn't, HR perspective

**For Investors:**

Decision: Invest in opportunity X
- Scenario plan: Returns 3x (30%), returns 1.5x (40%), lose investment (30%)
- Expected value: 30%×3x + 40%×1.5x + 30%×0x = 1.5x expected return
- Backcast: Returned 3x - why? Market timing right, team executed, competitive landscape favorable
- Premortem: Lost investment - why? Market turned, team conflict, competitor disrupted, regulatory change
- Calibration: Track all investments with initial probability estimates
- Truth-seeking pod: Include someone bearish on sector, someone bullish, neutral analyst

**For Product Managers:**

Decision: Build feature X vs. Y vs. Z
- Scenario plan: Each feature - adoption rate, impact on metrics, development time, opportunity cost
- Expected value: Impact × Probability × Time-discount - Cost
- Backcast: Feature X succeeded - why? Users wanted it, easy to find, solves real pain point
- Premortem: Feature X failed - why? Misunderstood user needs, too complex, poor discoverability
- Outcome fielding: If fails, was it execution or strategy? If succeeds, which assumptions were right?
- Truth-seeking pod: Include engineer (feasibility), designer (usability), customer-facing (needs), exec (strategy)

**For Personal Life:**

Decision: Take job offer vs. stay at current job
- Scenario plan: New job great (40%), new job okay (30%), new job poor (30%); staying great (20%), okay (50%), poor (30%)
- Backcast: New job great - why? Work is meaningful, colleagues excellent, comp fair, career growth
- Premortem: New job poor - why? Culture mismatch, promises not kept, role different than described, personal life suffers
- 10-10-10: How will I feel about this in 10 minutes? 10 months? 10 years?
- Truth-seeking pod: Include someone who changed jobs recently, someone who stayed put, mentor

### Mental Models Integration

**Thinking in Bets + First Principles:**
- Break decisions into fundamental truths
- Assign probabilities to each assumption
- Scenario plan around which assumptions might be wrong
- Backcast: Which assumptions held?
- Premortem: Which assumptions broke?

**Thinking in Bets + Second-Order Thinking:**
- Scenario plan: Not just immediate outcomes, but consequences of consequences
- Multi-level decision trees
- Backcast: Chain of events leading to success
- Premortem: Chain of events leading to failure

**Thinking in Bets + Inversion:**
- Premortem is inversion (avoid failure)
- Backcasting is forward (achieve success)
- Both necessary for complete picture
- Outcome fielding: What should I NOT have done? (inversion)

**Thinking in Bets + Margin of Safety:**
- Probability estimates include error bars
- Ulysses contracts create margin of safety
- Premortems identify where margin needed
- Expected value reduced by margin of safety factor

## Conclusion: The Long Game

### The Core Equation Revisited

Quality of life = Decision quality + Luck

We can't control luck. But we can dramatically improve decision quality by:

1. **Recognizing uncertainty** - Life is poker, not chess
2. **Avoiding resulting** - Judge process, not outcome
3. **Thinking probabilistically** - Express confidence numerically
4. **Using groups** - Truth-seeking pods combat bias
5. **Mental time travel** - Past and future inform present
6. **Scenario planning** - Prepare for multiple futures
7. **Backcasting** - Work backward from success
8. **Premortems** - Work backward from failure
9. **Outcome fielding** - Separate luck from skill
10. **Calibrating beliefs** - Track and improve accuracy

### The Humble Goal

Not to become perfectly rational (impossible), but to:
- Be a little less biased
- Make a few better decisions
- Catch a few more learning opportunities
- Improve calibration gradually
- Treat ourselves with more compassion

### Small Improvements Compound

Like a ship's navigation: A small course correction now prevents being miles off target later.

**The Payoff (Not Guaranteed, But More Likely):**
- More accurate beliefs
- Better decisions
- Appropriate confidence
- Less regret
- More self-compassion
- Deeper understanding
- Continuous improvement
- Greater happiness over time

### Starting Point

You don't need to implement everything at once:

**Week 1:** Just start expressing uncertainty with numbers
**Week 2:** Add one mental time travel technique
**Week 3:** Find two people for a truth-seeking pod
**Week 4:** Do one premortem

Small steps, compounding over time.

### Final Word

This methodology doesn't promise perfection or certainty. It promises something better: a framework for navigating uncertainty with clearer vision, better judgment, and greater self-awareness.

The goal isn't to win every bet—that's impossible. The goal is to make increasingly better bets, learn from all outcomes (wins and losses), and improve our decision-making process over time.

As poker and life teach us: It's all one long game. The question is whether we'll play it with eyes open or closed.
