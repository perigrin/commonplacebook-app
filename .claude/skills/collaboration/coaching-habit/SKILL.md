# The Coaching Habit: Seven Questions That Change Everything

A practical coaching framework from Michael Bungay Stanier built around seven essential questions. This is **coaching for the busy manager**: informal, daily, 10 minutes or less, not formal coaching sessions.

**Core Shift**: Tell less, ask more. Your advice isn't as good as you think it is.

## The Three Vicious Cycles This Breaks

1. **Creating Overdependence**: Teams become excessively reliant on you for solutions
2. **Getting Overwhelmed**: You drown in responsibilities and lose focus
3. **Becoming Disconnected**: Work loses both impact and meaning

## The Two Types of Coaching

- **Coaching for Performance**: Addressing the specific problem (putting out the fire)
- **Coaching for Development**: Focusing on the person, not just the issue (managing the fire-fighter)

**More powerful conversations happen at the development level** where learning, growth, and capability expansion occur.

## THE 7 ESSENTIAL QUESTIONS

### 1. The Kickstart Question: "What's on your mind?"

**Purpose**: Start conversations quickly and get to what matters most

**Why it works**:
- Open enough to let them choose what's important
- Focused enough to avoid rambling
- Dissolves ossified agendas, sidesteps small talk
- Gets to real issues in 60 seconds

**The 3P Model** (use to deepen the conversation):

After asking "What's on your mind?", clarify focus:
- **Projects**: The content/situation (coaching for performance)
- **People**: Relationships and your role (not gossip about others)
- **Patterns**: Behaviors you'd like to change (coaching for development)

**Traps it prevents**:
- Small Talk Tango (wasting time on trivia)
- Ossified Agenda (same tired meeting structure)
- Default Diagnosis (assuming you know the problem)

---

### 2. The AWE Question: "And what else?"

**Status**: **The Best Coaching Question in the World**

**Purpose**: Generate more options, tame your Advice Monster, buy yourself time

**Why it works**:
- First answers are rarely the only or best answers
- Having multiple options reduces decision failure from 52% to 30%
- Stops you from jumping in with advice prematurely
- Keeps them generating ideas instead of you

**How to use it**:
- Ask it **at least 3 times, rarely more than 5**
- Stay genuinely curious when asking
- Listen to the answers (bonus points for this)
- Stop when they say "There is nothing else" (that's success)
- Use "Is there anything else?" as the closing variation

**Pairs with everything**:
- "What's on your mind?" → "And what else?"
- "What could you do?" → "And what else could you do?"
- "What's the real challenge?" → "And what else is a challenge?"

**The Paradox of Choice**: Don't go overboard - aim for 3-5 options, not dozens.

---

### 3. The Focus Question: "What's the real challenge here for you?"

**Purpose**: Stop solving the wrong problem; get to the heart of the issue

**Why it's phrased this way**:
- "What's the challenge?" = Too vague
- "What's the real challenge here?" = Slows them down, makes them think deeper
- "What's the real challenge here **for you**?" = Makes it personal and developmental

**The Three Foggy-fiers it defeats**:

1. **Proliferation of Challenges**: Problems pour out like popcorn
   - *Say*: "If you had to pick one to focus on, which would be the real challenge for you?"

2. **Coaching the Ghost**: Talking about another person/project instead of their role
   - *Say*: "I understand what's going on with [person]. What's the real challenge here for you?"

3. **Abstractions & Generalizations**: High-level discussion with no personal connection
   - *Say*: "I have a sense of the overall challenge. What's the real challenge here for you?"

**Key insight**: You can only coach the person in front of you. Make it about THEIR challenge, THEIR role, THEIR growth.

**Power move**: Add "for you" to any question to make it more developmental. Research shows people solve problems faster when "you" is in the question.

---

### 4. The Foundation Question: "What do you want?"

**Purpose**: Create adult-to-adult relationships; clarify mutual expectations

**Why it's difficult**:
- We often don't know what we actually want
- Even when we know, we're afraid to ask for it
- Even when we ask, we hide it in rhetoric

**Wants vs. Needs** (from Nonviolent Communication):
- **Wants**: Surface requests, tactical (the what)
- **Needs**: Deeper human drivers (the why)

**The 9 Universal Needs** (Manfred Max-Neef):
- Affection, Creation, Recreation
- Freedom, Identity, Understanding
- Participation, Protection, Subsistence

**Example**: "I want you to talk to the VP for me"
- Possible need: Protection (I'm too junior) or Participation (I need you to do your part)

**Power move**: Share what YOU want too. Create an exchange of value.

**The TERA Quotient** (Neuroscience):

Your brain scans 5 times per second asking: "Is it safe here?"

Four drivers:
- **T = Tribe**: Are you with me or against me?
- **E = Expectation**: Do I know what's coming?
- **R = Rank**: Am I important in this moment?
- **A = Autonomy**: Do I get a say?

Asking "What do you want?" increases TERA by building tribe, increasing autonomy, and raising their rank.

---

### 5. The Lazy Question: "How can I help?"

**Purpose**: Stop rescuing; clarify what's needed; avoid wasted effort

**The Drama Triangle** (Stephen Karpman):

We play three dysfunctional roles when triggered:

**Victim**
- Belief: "My life is hard. Poor me."
- Dynamic: "It's not my fault (it's theirs)"
- Stuck feeling: "I feel useless"

**Persecutor**
- Belief: "I'm surrounded by fools"
- Dynamic: "It's not my fault (it's yours)"
- Stuck feeling: "I don't trust anyone"

**Rescuer** (Most common for managers)
- Belief: "Let me jump in and fix it"
- Dynamic: "It's my responsibility (not yours)"
- Stuck feeling: "My rescuing doesn't work"

**Problem**: Rescuing disempowers, creates dependency, overwhelms you.

**How "How can I help?" works**:
1. Forces them to make a direct, clear request
2. Stops you from assuming you know how to help
3. Keeps you curious and lazy (self-management tool)

**Your range of responses**:
- "Yes" (you can always say yes)
- "No, I can't do that" (you don't have to say yes)
- "I can't do that... but I could do [counter-offer]"
- "Let me think about that" (buy time)

**Anti-Rescuer Habit**:

When they ask "How do I...?" or "What should I do...?"

**INSTEAD OF** giving the answer:

**SAY**: "Great question. I've got some ideas. But before I share, what are your first thoughts?"

They answer → "That's terrific. What else could you do?"

They answer → "This is all good. Anything else?"

*THEN* add your idea if you wish.

---

### 6. The Strategic Question: "If you're saying Yes to this, what are you saying No to?"

**Purpose**: Force clarity on commitments; create focus; prevent overwhelm

**Core principle**: "The essence of strategy is choosing what NOT to do." (Michael Porter)

**Why it works**:
- A Yes is nothing without the No that gives it boundaries
- Too easy to shove another Yes into an overcommitted life
- Forces trade-offs and tough choices

**Two types of No**:

1. **No of Omission**: What's automatically eliminated?
   - Understanding implications and opportunity cost

2. **No of Commission**: What do you need to say No to to make this Yes happen?
   - Creating space, focus, energy, resources

**Use the 3P Model to find the Nos**:

**Projects**: What projects do you abandon? What meetings will you skip?

**People**: What expectations do you manage? What Drama Triangle dynamics will you extract yourself from?

**Patterns**: What habits do you break? What old stories do you update?

**How to say No when you can't say No (Part 1): Say Yes More Slowly**

Stay curious before committing:
- Why are you asking me?
- Who else have you asked?
- When you say urgent, what do you mean?
- According to what standard? By when?
- If I could only do part, what part?
- What do you want me to take off my plate?

**How to say No when you can't say No (Part 2): Create a Third Point**

Say Yes to the person, No to the task. Write the request down, point to it: "I have to say No to this" (vs. "No to you").

---

### 7. The Learning Question: "What was most useful for you?"

**Purpose**: Create learning moments; embed insights; finish strong

**Core principle**: People learn when they recall and reflect, not when you tell them.

**Double-Loop Learning** (Chris Argyris):
- First loop: Fix the problem
- Second loop: Create learning (where neural pathways form)

**Why this exact question**:

1. **Assumes useful**: Frames what happened as valuable
2. **Asks for One Big Thing**: What's most worth remembering
3. **Makes it personal**: "for you" - subjective, not abstract
4. **Gives you feedback**: What to do more of next time
5. **It's learning, not judgment**: Not "Was this useful?" but "What was useful?"
6. **Reminds them you're useful**: Every conversation ends with them articulating value

**The Neuroscience (AGES Model)**:
- **A**ttention
- **G**eneration (creating your own connections - this question hits here)
- **E**motion
- **S**pacing

When people generate their own answers vs. being told, memory retention increases substantially.

**The Peak-End Rule** (Kahneman):

Experiences are judged by:
- The peak/trough moment
- The ending moments

Finishing on "this was useful" makes them remember the entire experience favorably.

**Power move**: Share what YOU found most useful too. Equal exchange strengthens the relationship.

## THE COACHING BOOKENDS

**START**: "What's on your mind?" (gets to what matters fast)

**END**: "What was most useful for you?" (extracts wisdom and embeds learning)

**Between them**: Use the other five questions as needed.

---

## THE IRRESISTIBLE 1-2-3 COMBINATION

The first three questions create a robust coaching script:

1. **Open**: "What's on your mind?"
2. **Check**: "Is there anything else on your mind?"
3. **Focus**: "So what's the real challenge here for you?"
4. **Expand**: "And what else (is the real challenge)?"
5. **Probe**: "Is there anything else?"
6. **Drill**: "So... what's the REAL challenge here for you?"

This sequence handles most coaching conversations.

## THE ADVICE MONSTER

**What it is**: The deeply ingrained habit of jumping into answer-it/solve-it/fix-it mode

**Why it's so strong**:
- Organizations reward answers and certainty
- Anxiety in modern work
- Brains prefer clarity and certainty
- You've been promoted for giving good advice
- Giving advice feels like "adding value"

**The trap**: Even though you don't really know what the issue is, you're quite sure you've got the answer.

**Research**: Doctors interrupt patients in 18 seconds. Managers aren't much better.

**If this were a haiku**:
*Tell less and ask more.*
*Your advice is not as good*
*As you think it is.*

**Taming it**:
- Ask "And what else?" to keep yourself quiet
- Use "How can I help?" as self-management
- Remember: There IS a place for advice - just not as your default

---

## WHEN TO USE COACHING VS. ADVICE-GIVING

**Use Coaching When**:
- You want to build self-sufficiency and autonomy
- There's time for them to figure it out
- The learning is more valuable than the immediate solution
- It's a pattern worth addressing, not a one-off
- You want to develop the person, not just solve the problem

**Give Advice When**:
- They ask "Where's the folder?" (Just tell them)
- Genuine urgency with no time for exploration
- You have critical information they lack
- They've already explored options thoroughly
- Safety or compliance is at stake

**Key**: The goal isn't to never give advice. It's to stop defaulting to advice when questions would be more effective.

## BUILDING THE COACHING HABIT

**The New Habit Formula** (5 Components):

### 1. Make a Vow (The Reason)
- Why bother changing?
- Connect to serving others
- Think about who it helps, not what it does for you

### 2. Figure Your Trigger (When This Happens...)
- Be specific: location, time, emotional state, other people, preceding action
- Example: "When Jenny asks for feedback in the team meeting" (not just "at meetings")

### 3. Double-S It: Be Short & Specific (I Will...)
- Define as micro-habit <60 seconds
- Each of the 7 questions fits this perfectly

### 4. Practice Deeply
- Repetition, repetition, repetition
- Notice when it goes well - celebrate success

### 5. Plan How to Get Back on Track (Instead Of...)
- Know your old habit you're stopping
- Know what you'll do when you stumble

**The Formula Template**:

**WHEN THIS HAPPENS** (trigger): _____________

**INSTEAD OF** (old habit): _____________

**I WILL** (new habit <60 seconds): _____________

## PRACTICAL APPLICATION TIPS

### Ask One Question at a Time
Don't pile on multiple questions. Let each breathe.

### Cut the Intro and Ask the Question
Don't preamble. Don't explain. Just ask.

### Should You Ask Rhetorical Questions?
No. Ask real questions that require real answers.

### Stick to Questions Starting with "What"
"What" questions are more effective than "Why" (which can feel accusatory).

### Get Comfortable with Silence
After asking, pause. Let them think. Don't fill the silence.

### Actually Listen to the Answer
Don't use their talking time to formulate your next brilliant response.

### Use Every Channel
Works in person, via email, IM, phone - everywhere.

## COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID

1. **Asking but not listening** → Practice genuine curiosity
2. **Asking too many questions at once** → One at a time
3. **Asking "And what else?" too many times** → 3-5 is sweet spot
4. **Solving the first problem mentioned** → Ask "What's the real challenge for you?" first
5. **Coaching the ghost** → Redirect to "What's the challenge for YOU?"
6. **Jumping into Rescuer mode** → Ask "How can I help?" and remember you can say No
7. **Saying Yes without understanding the No** → "If Yes, what are you saying No to?"
8. **Ending without capturing learning** → Always ask "What was most useful?"
9. **Being mechanical** → Stay genuinely curious; these are tools, not scripts
10. **Giving up when awkward** → Expect awkwardness, start small, get back on the horse
